## Claim

A magnetometer array can recover an evoked C-fibre magnetic signal from a
superficial human nerve if, and only if, it rejects cardiac, drift and mains
interference by roughly an order of magnitude. Improving sensor sensitivity
below about 1 fT/√Hz buys nothing, because sensor noise is not what is in the
way. The relevant engineering is a **gradiometric configuration with reference
sensors and adaptive cancellation**, not a quieter magnetometer.

**Scope, stated here rather than in the mechanism so it cannot be mistaken for
part of it:** the sensors are quantum devices; no claim is made that anything
quantum happens inside the nerve. This is a Branch B instrument conjecture and
its mechanism is classical electromagnetism and array signal processing.

## Why this, why now

This conjecture was generated by the failure of its parent. C-004 proposed
recovering the signal by velocity-domain matched filtering and was refuted by
simulation. But the refutation was itself twice corrected, and what survived is
a specific, quantitative redirection.

**What the C-004 simulation established, over 18 seeds** (results in
`simulations/C-004-velocity-beamforming/results/sensor-realism/ENSEMBLE.md`):

| Condition | Detectability ratio, mean | 95% CI | Seeds above threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sensor noise only, 1 fT/√Hz alkali OPM | **1.246** | [1.178, 1.314] | 18 / 18 |
| Plus cardiac, drift and mains interference | **0.731** | [0.649, 0.814] | 1 / 18 |
| Plus a 50/100/150 Hz notch | **0.783** | [0.698, 0.869] | 2 / 18 |

Two things follow, and both were surprises.

**The bandwidth objection is dead.** C-band signal energy is 50% below 2.8 Hz,
90% below 29.7 Hz and 99% below 106 Hz, against Aβ at 90% below 557 Hz. The
kilohertz requirement in E-04 is real for *myelinated* volleys and was wrongly
carried over to unmyelinated ones. A 350 Hz alkali magnetometer has ample
bandwidth for C-fibre traffic.

**And the velocity-domain method works.** Against sensor noise alone it
recovers the ridge in every one of 18 seeds. C-004's signal-processing idea was
not the problem.

The problem is interference, and notching the mains recovers only a fifth of
the shortfall, which says the dominant term is **cardiac and drift**, not mains.

**Why not already done.** This is not a new technique. Gradiometry and
reference-array regression are how OPM-MEG achieves usable signal-to-noise
against environmental fields thousands of times larger than the brain signal,
and magnetospinography systems already use multichannel arrays. What has not
been done is applying that machinery to the specific problem of a slow,
dispersed, unmyelinated volley, because nobody has tried to record one, because
everyone believed the sensitivity was the barrier.

## Mechanism

Interference and signal differ in **spatial structure**, which is what a
gradiometer exploits and a single magnetometer cannot.

Cardiac fields at a limb sensor arrive from a distant source, so they are
spatially near-uniform across a 10 cm array: the field difference between
adjacent sensors is small compared with the field itself. Environmental drift
and mains are, to first order, uniform for the same reason. A nerve volley 6.5
mm beneath one sensor is the opposite: it is a local source whose field falls
steeply with distance and whose spatial pattern moves along the array at the
conduction velocity.

A first-order gradiometer measures the difference between two sensors, which
suppresses the common-mode term by the ratio of source distances while
preserving the local one. Reference-array regression generalises it: measure
the interference somewhere the nerve signal cannot reach, fit it, subtract it.

Timescales and amplitudes are ordinary and already stated: signal about 1 pT
at 6.5 mm for a synchronous myelinated volley, C-band content below about
106 Hz, cardiac at roughly 1 Hz, mains at 50 Hz, drift below 10 Hz. This is
array signal processing on classical electromagnetism throughout.

## Forbidden observation

Adding gradiometric or reference-array interference rejection to the C-004
pipeline will not raise the C-band detectability ratio above 1.0 in the
presence of realistic cardiac, drift and mains interference.

## Killer

Extend the existing simulation, which already contains the forward model, the
beamformer, the interference generators and the null-distribution machinery.

Add: a reference sensor at a standoff where the nerve contribution is
negligible; first-order and second-order gradiometer configurations; and
reference-regression cancellation. Then re-run the full pipeline with
interference present, across **at least 18 seeds**, and report the
detectability ratio as a mean with a confidence interval rather than a single
draw.

**Refutation threshold:** the conjecture is refuted if the mean detectability
ratio across seeds remains below 1.0 with a 95% confidence interval excluding
1.0, with interference present and rejection applied, while the Aβ positive
control still passes for the same configuration.

Approximate cost: **a day of compute and no money.** That is the point. If it
survives in simulation it justifies an instrumentation proposal; if it fails,
Branch B closes for good and this time for a reason that is not an artefact of
the wrong assumption.

## Rivals

- **The interference cannot be separated because it is not spatially uniform
  enough.** At a limb, cardiac and muscular fields may have enough spatial
  gradient across 10 cm that common-mode rejection removes the signal along
  with the noise. *Distinguished by:* the Aβ positive control. If gradiometry
  suppresses the known large fast component too, the configuration is wrong
  rather than the conjecture.
- **The dominant interference is muscular, not cardiac, and is local.**
  Electromyographic sources in the limb itself are close to the sensors and
  would not be rejected as common mode. This is the most likely way the
  conjecture fails and the simulation does not currently model muscle at all.
  *Distinguished by:* adding a local muscular interference source and seeing
  whether rejection still works.
- **Sensitivity does matter after all, and the 1 fT/√Hz figure is optimistic
  for a fielded instrument.** Bench sensitivity and in-situ sensitivity differ,
  and a real array at a limb may not reach it. *Distinguished by:* re-running
  the sweep with rejection applied to find what sensitivity is then required,
  which converts the question into a procurement specification.

## Severity

Given the conjecture is false, the probability the proposed test still comes
out favourable is about **0.2**.

The main route to a false pass is that a cancellation scheme with enough free
parameters will subtract anything, including the signal, and then find
structure in the residual. Three controls: the Aβ ridge must survive the same
rejection, the null distribution is computed through the identical rejection
pipeline, and the ensemble is reported as a distribution rather than a single
run. That last one is not hypothetical caution; the parent conjecture's
sensor-realism run printed a confident verdict from a 1-in-18 outlier.

## What it would change

If confirmed, the instrument that would read nociceptive traffic
non-invasively is buildable now from commercial components, and the
specification is a reference-array geometry rather than an exotic sensor. That
is a proposal a group like Bu and Lerman's, who already recorded 1 pT median
nerve signals with three OPMs, could act on.

If refuted with the Aβ control intact, **Branch B closes**, and it closes on a
quantitative statement rather than a hunch: the interference floor at a human
limb is above the C-fibre signal by a stated margin, and no arrangement of
current sensors bridges it. That would also settle HC-2's engineering half and
leave HC-2 standing or falling purely on the physiology.

Either outcome is worth a day of compute, which is the strongest thing that can
be said for a conjecture in this programme.
