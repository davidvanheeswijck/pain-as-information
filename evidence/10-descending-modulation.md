## Bottom line

The owner's question was whether the pain-modulation system could be "a key
into regulating pain". The honest answer from the literature is: **the system
is real, the test for it is weak, and the two findings that made it famous
have not replicated cleanly.** That is a statement about those two specific
claims. Pooled across musculoskeletal pain studies, baseline CPM does
correlate with later pain, mean r = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20-0.50; PMID 31045746),
so CPM carries some prognostic signal at group level.

1. **Descending modulation is established physiology.** A loop from the dorsal
   horn up to the brainstem and back down inhibits or facilitates the spinal
   neurons that relay pain. It was described in the rat in 1979 and shown to
   need supraspinal structures (PMIDs 460935, 460936), and it has an opioid
   link in humans (PMID 2168836).
2. **The conditioned pain modulation (CPM) test is a group-level instrument,
   not an individual one.** The pooled between-session reliability is "fair or
   less for all modalities" (PMID 35142147), agreement on who counts as a
   responder between two days is poor (PMID 30007061), and some healthy people
   show facilitation instead of inhibition (PMID 30699074).
3. **The two headline prediction claims are single-group findings that later
   independent work has not supported.** Pre-operative CPM predicting chronic
   post-thoracotomy pain (n=62, PMID 18079062) has no independent replication
   in thoracic surgery. A larger independent lung-surgery cohort that included
   CPM in its test battery found no pre-operative QST difference
   (PMID 37327358). Its abstract gives no CPM-specific result, and it used a
   different CPM method and a different definition of chronic pain, so it is
   a failure to find, not a clean null test of CPM. CPM predicting duloxetine benefit in
   painful diabetic neuropathy (n=30, PMID 22480803) has no independent
   replication found by these queries, and the nearest independent trials point
   the other way or find nothing (PMIDs 37994185, 42956041).
4. **In CRPS the brake is reported intact, in two small cohorts.** Two
   independent groups, one in early disease (n=24) and one in chronic disease
   (n=16), found CPM not reduced against healthy controls; in the early cohort
   it was, if anything, larger (PMIDs 28146319, 41244943). **Reading:** on
   present evidence a "broken brake" is not the explanation of CRPS pain at
   group level, and 40 patients cannot exclude a subgroup in whom it is.
5. **For spinal cord stimulation, baseline CPM has been tied to outcome in
   three small studies, in opposite directions.** For dorsal root ganglion
   stimulation, **no study testing baseline CPM or QST as a predictor of
   response or of fading benefit was found by these queries** (appendix
   Q36, Q44, Q91 to Q97).
6. **Genetics does not single out descending-modulation cells.** The largest
   cell-type analysis reports enrichment in cortical and limbic glutamatergic
   neurons and in one nociceptor subtype of the human ganglion
   (PMID 41055971). Its abstract names no brainstem modulatory nucleus.
   Whether such nuclei were tested at all is not known from the abstract.

So descending modulation is a real mechanism and a poor biomarker. It is a
legitimate rival explanation for things the programme has so far attributed to
the periphery or to the dorsal horn alone. It is not, on this evidence, a
handle that is ready to be turned.

## 1. What descending pain modulation is, and what the tests measure

### 1.1 In plain terms

Pain signals from the body arrive at the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and are
relayed upward. The relay is not a fixed wire. Nerve fibres that run *down*
from the brainstem end on the same relay neurons and can turn their output down
(a brake) or up (an accelerator). How much pain a given input produces
therefore depends partly on a setting that comes from above, and that setting
changes with attention, expectation, sleep, drugs and disease.

The current review of the circuit's main output station describes it this way:
the rostral ventromedial medulla "integrates 'top-down' and 'bottom-up' inputs
that regulate functionally defined RVM cell types, 'OFF-cells' and 'ON-cells',
which respectively suppress or facilitate pain-related sensory processing"
(De Preter & Heinricher, *Trends Neurosci* 2024, PMID 38749825). The same
abstract says this understanding is "derived primarily from rodent studies but
with corroborating evidence from human imaging". The brake and the accelerator
are both part of the normal system. "Descending modulation" does not mean
"descending inhibition".

### 1.2 The founding experiment

Le Bars, Dickenson and Besson recorded 68 convergent (wide-dynamic-range)
dorsal horn neurons in anaesthetised rats. A noxious stimulus applied anywhere
else on the body "powerfully inhibited" 67 of the 68, and it depressed their
C-fibre responses "by 60-100%". Non-noxious stimuli did not do this, hence the
name diffuse noxious inhibitory controls, DNIC (PMID 460935). Two details of
the companion paper matter for this programme (PMID 460936):

- DNIC had "no effect" on noxious-only, non-noxious and proprioceptive dorsal
  horn cells. The abstract concludes "convergent neurones are specifically
  inhibited by DNIC".
- DNIC "could not be demonstrated for convergent neurones in the spinal
  animal. Thus the neuronal substrate for DNIC must involve supraspinal
  structures."

In humans, a painful heat stimulus to one hand "strongly depressed" a spinal
withdrawal reflex evoked at the opposite ankle, in nine subjects, and the
effect was "completely blocked" by an opioid antagonist in a double-blind
crossover (Willer, Le Bars & De Broucker, PMID 2168836). Functional imaging in
54 healthy people linked the analgesia to signal changes in three brainstem
regions including "the region of the subnucleus reticularis dorsalis"; note
that in the same study 23 subjects showed the analgesia and 31 did not
(Youssef, Macefield & Henderson, PMID 26343321).

### 1.3 What the CPM test actually measures

Conditioned pain modulation is the human behavioural version of DNIC. A *test
stimulus* (pressure, heat or an electrical pulse) is rated or thresholded
alone, then again during or just after a second, painful *conditioning
stimulus* at a distant site, usually a hand in cold water. The CPM effect is
the difference between the two. If the test stimulus hurts less with the
conditioning stimulus present, CPM is called "efficient" or "inhibitory".

The name was fixed by a consensus letter (Yarnitsky et al., *Eur J Pain* 2010,
PMID 20227310; **no abstract exists in PubMed, title only read**), so that
"DNIC" refers to the animal neuronal mechanism and "CPM" to the human
psychophysical test.

What the number is, and is not:

- It is **a difference between two pain ratings or thresholds, given by a
  person who knows a second painful stimulus is being applied.** It is not a
  recording of any descending pathway.
- It is a **net** effect. Facilitation and inhibition both contribute, and a
  small CPM can mean weak inhibition or strong facilitation.
- It is **open to expectation and attention** (§5.3). A meta-analysis found CPM
  "is more efficient when attention is directed towards the CS versus the TS",
  with evidence it called "limited and conflicting" (PMID 39541722).
- **A non-inhibitory result is not automatically abnormal.** In 146 pain-free
  people, "a subset of subjects displayed a decrease in thresholds during the
  conditioning stimulus", which "produced negative values for most of the
  computed percentiles" (PMID 30699074). In 136 pain-free people tested for
  CPM, a permutation method sorted individuals into inhibitory responders,
  non-responders and facilitatory responders (PMID 40692375; the abstract gives
  no percentages for CPM).

### 1.4 How CPM relates to quantitative sensory testing

Quantitative sensory testing (QST) is the wider family: calibrated stimuli and
a person's report. The reference protocol is that of the German Research
Network on Neuropathic Pain (DFNS), which gives "a complete profile for one
region within 30 min", with reference values from 180 healthy subjects, and
includes thermal and mechanical detection and pain thresholds, dynamic
mechanical allodynia and "pain summation (wind-up ratio)" (Rolke et al.,
PMID 16697110).

The useful distinction is between **static** and **dynamic** measures:

| | What it asks | Examples | In the DFNS battery |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static QST | Where is the threshold? | heat, cold, pressure and pinprick thresholds | yes |
| Dynamic QST, facilitatory | Does repeated input build up? | temporal summation, wind-up ratio | wind-up ratio only |
| Dynamic QST, inhibitory | Does a second pain turn the first down? | **CPM**, offset analgesia | **no** |

CPM is therefore an add-on to the DFNS battery and not part of it, going by
the list of tests in that protocol's abstract. Other QST batteries may
include it. The NeuPSIG
consensus lists "the study of conditioned pain modulation" among the promising
applications of QST, and states that QST "is not recommended as a stand-alone
test for the diagnosis of neuropathic pain" (Backonja et al., PMID 23742795).

## 2. How reliable is CPM?

Short answer: **good enough to compare groups, not good enough to classify a
person.** The disagreement between the two systematic reviews below is mostly a
disagreement about which of those two jobs is being asked of the test.

### 2.1 Test-retest reliability

kappa, which are agreement statistics on a 0 to 1 scale. They are not effect
sizes.

The two reviews reach different headline conclusions. Kennedy 2016:
"Conditioned pain modulation is a reliable measure; however, the degree of
reliability is heavily dependent on stimulation parameters and study
methodology". Nuwailati 2022: "The inter-session reliability was fair or less
for all modalities, both in healthy volunteers and chronic pain patients."
The later one pooled more studies and is the one to anchor on. Note that
Kennedy 2016 has an author from the group that made the two best-known
prediction claims (§3), and Nuwailati 2022 does not.

**Reading:** the one pooled patient figure, 0.44 with a confidence interval
from 0.11 to 0.69, rests on a single protocol. For the population this
programme cares about, between-session reliability of CPM is close to
unmeasured.

### 2.2 The responder problem

An ICC describes how well a test ranks people. Most proposed uses of CPM need
something harder: sorting one person into "efficient" or "inefficient". When
studies ask that question, using the measurement error of the test stimulus as
the yardstick, the picture is worse.

- In 50 healthy volunteers, an inhibitory CPM effect beyond twice the standard
  error of measurement "was elicited in 59% of subjects in response to cold
  stimulus; in 44% to heat" (PMID 31715262). The authors' summary: "CPM effect
  was paradigm dependent at intrasession testing and unstable to the same
  paradigm at intersession testing."
- Across ten protocols in 26 healthy men, "a large proportion (from 11.5 to
  73.1%) of subjects was classified as CPM non-responders" (PMID 30007061).
  The protocol with the best day-to-day agreement "also failed to induce a CPM
  response in more than half of the sample", and the commonly used pressure
  plus cold-water protocol "induced a CPM effect in most participants however
  it was inconsistent in doing so."

These are two different percentages of two different things, in two different
samples, and they are not a range. What they share is the conclusion.

### 2.3 Protocol dependence

Every element of the protocol moves the result:

- **Test stimulus and conditioning stimulus.** Pressure as the test stimulus
  with cold water as the conditioning stimulus were "most consistently
  associated with good to excellent intra-session reliability"
  (PMID 35142147).
- **Timing.** Testing *during* conditioning (parallel) gives larger effects but
  is "confounded by divided attention"; Lin et al. "recommend a sequential
  protocol" (PMID 42080990). A randomised crossover in 72 healthy adults
  found that focusing attention on the conditioning stimulus made CPM smaller,
  and its authors advise against rating the conditioning pain while it is
  applied (PMID 40542602).
- **How the effect is calculated.** Subtracting a no-conditioning control
  session, which is the more defensible calculation, lowered reliability to
  poor-to-fair in both protocols tested (PMID 33065759).
- **Who is tested.** A meta-analysis of 30 patient-versus-control studies found
  "a significant influence of participant gender and age on the effect size"
  (PMID 22981090).

### 2.4 Consensus recommendations

Two consensus documents exist, and this brief could read neither in full.

- **Yarnitsky et al., "Recommendations on terminology and practice of
  psychophysical DNIC testing", *Eur J Pain* 2010, PMID 20227310.** A letter
  with no abstract.
- **Yarnitsky et al., "Recommendations on practice of conditioned pain
  modulation (CPM) testing", *Eur J Pain* 2015, PMID 25330039.** Thirteen
  authors from a "researchers consensus meeting". The abstract says only that
  "Protocols for testing conditioned pain modulation (CPM) vary between
  different labs/clinics" and that the paper summarises the meeting's
  recommendations on practice and reporting. **The recommendations themselves
  are `[UNVERIFIED]` here**: the paper is not open access and the publisher
  page returned HTTP 403 on 18 November 2026. No specific recommendation
  should be attributed to it on the strength of this brief.

What can be said from abstracts is where the field stood afterwards. Six years
after the 2015 paper: "Reporting in conditioned pain modulation (CPM) studies
is not standardised" and "Recommendation is made for the performance of a
multicentre, test-retest study" (PMID 33065759). Eleven years after it: "the
lack of protocol standardization and reference data prevents clinical use in
individual patients" (PMID 42080990). A 2025 systematic review of reflex-based
CPM protocols found "significant methodological heterogeneity" across 16
studies and issued its own GRADE-based recommendations (PMID 41144904).

**Reading:** the consensus process produced a shared name and a reporting
standard. It did not produce a shared protocol, and three later papers from
three groups say so.

### 2.5 Does CPM track clinical pain at all?

Two findings that sit awkwardly together:

- Across 30 studies, 778 patients and 664 controls, "there was a large effect
  size of .78, reflecting reduced conditioned pain modulation in the patient
  group", with the caveat that "poor blinding of assessors and a lack of
  control of confounding variables were common" (Lewis, Rice & McNair,
  PMID 22981090). This is an effect size for the difference between groups;
  the abstract does not name the statistic.
- Within patients, across 32 studies and 1958 patients, "the majority of the
  results (69%) reported nonsignificant correlations between CPM efficiency
  and clinical manifestations of pain" (Fernandes et al., PMID 31365469).
  This is a proportion of reported correlations, not of patients.

**Reading:** CPM separates patient groups from control groups on average and
mostly fails to track how much pain an individual patient has. That is the
profile of a marker that reflects something about having chronic pain, or about
being a patient in a pain study, and it is weaker support for CPM as a measure
of the mechanism that sets pain intensity.

## 3. Does CPM predict anything?

Two claims carry this field: that CPM measured before surgery predicts who
gets chronic pain afterwards, and that CPM measured before treatment predicts
who benefits from a drug. Each is taken in turn, with every study's group,
size and result.

**The grading used below is this brief's own**, and is a reading:

- **A**: prospective, adequately sized, and reproduced by a group sharing no
  authors with the original.
- **B**: prospective positive finding, with independent results that are mixed.
- **C**: a single study or a single research lineage, not independently
  reproduced.
- **D**: independent attempts exist and the weight of them is null or
  opposite.

"Independent" was judged from the author list and the first and last author
affiliations in each PubMed record. Shared co-authors are named where found.

### 3.1 Chronic pain after surgery

#### The founding claim

> **Yarnitsky D, Crispel Y, Eisenberg E, Granovsky Y, Ben-Nun A, Sprecher E,
> Best LA, Granot M. "Prediction of chronic post-operative pain: pre-operative
> DNIC testing identifies patients at risk." *Pain* 2008;138(1):22-28.
> PMID 18079062.** Haifa.

62 patients tested before thoracotomy and followed "29.0+/-16.9 weeks".
"Efficient DNIC predicted lower risk of CPTP, with OR 0.52 (0.33-0.77 95% CI,
p=0.0024), i.e., a 10-point numerical pain scale (NPS) reduction halves the
chance to develop chronic pain." This is an odds ratio per 10 points of CPM
effect from a logistic regression, in a single-centre cohort. Pain thresholds
and supra-threshold ratings "did not predict CPTP".

#### Every prospective study found, by surgery

Three things in this table need saying out loud.

**The founding result has not been reproduced in thoracic surgery by an
independent group.** The two later chest-surgery cohorts, both Danish and
neither sharing an author with the Haifa paper, did not find it
(PMIDs 24587268, 37327358). They are not exact replications. One is funnel
chest repair in healthy males and reports a CPM-specific null in 31 followed
patients. The other mixes thoracoscopic and open lung surgery, lost 40% to
follow-up, measured CPM by cuff algometry where the original used a fall in
pain ratings, counted pain of any intensity as chronic pain, and reports only
that no pre-operative QST value differed. Its abstract gives no CPM-specific
figure, so it is a failure to find any QST difference in a study that included
CPM, and not a clean null test of CPM. They are the nearest tests that exist,
and the larger one has about twice the original's sample.

**The one clean independent positive is in a different operation.** Dürsteler
et al. in Barcelona share no author with the Haifa group, registered the study
prospectively, and found a difference in persistent-pain proportions of about
20 percentage points (PMID 33817538). Against it, the largest knee cohort,
also independent, did not keep CPM in its model (PMID 30236242, n=300).

**A large share of this literature comes from one network.** Eight of the
twelve rows have an Aalborg affiliation or co-author. This is not a criticism
of the work, and several of those rows are nulls that the same people
published. It does mean the rows are not twelve independent draws.

#### What the systematic reviews conclude

Four reviews, none of which pooled CPM into a single estimate for surgery.
Each figure below is a count of studies, not of patients.

- **Sangesland, Støren & Vaegter 2017, PMID 28850344.** 30 studies, 2738
  subjects. "The majority of the preoperative QST variables showed no
  consistent association with pain intensity after surgery." The most
  consistent were supra-threshold heat pain and temporal summation of pressure
  pain. CPM is not named among them.
- **van Helmond et al. 2020, PMID 32925335.** 24 studies, 2732 subjects, 22 of
  24 at "moderate to high risk of bias in multiple quality domains". 14 studies
  (58%) reported a significant association for some QST measure; CPM accounted
  for 3 of them.
- **Petersen et al. 2021, PMID 32701654.** 25 surgical and 11 pharmacological
  studies. CPM was assessed in 16 studies and was associated with outcome in
  44% of them. That percentage mixes surgical and drug studies. The authors
  conclude that temporal summation and CPM "show the most consistent predictive
  values" and that "the heterogeneous methodologies reduce the
  generalizability".
- **Paredes et al. 2022, PMID 34224493.** Joint replacement only, 18 studies,
  1869 patients. CPM was associated with post-surgical pain "in 1 study (of 6,
  17%)". Conclusion: "The predictive role of presurgical QST for
  postarthroplasty pain remains unclear".

The 44% and the 17% are not in conflict and should not be averaged. They count
different sets of studies with different outcomes.

**Grade for "pre-operative CPM predicts chronic post-surgical pain": B, leaning
D for thoracic surgery specifically.** One independent positive in knee
replacement, no QST difference in the nearest independent study of the
original's setting (which did not report CPM separately), and
systematic reviews that find temporal summation more consistent than CPM.

#### Outside surgery

- **Central neuropathic pain after spinal cord injury.** In 47 people tested
  1.5 months after injury, those who later developed central neuropathic pain
  had "early, reduced at-level pain adaptation and CPM magnitudes"
  (Gruener et al., Tel Aviv, PMID 31693542). A separate Canadian cohort of 35
  found the opposite ordering in time: CPM fell between admission and
  discharge "only in patients already suffering from neuropathic pain at
  admission", and the authors read this as pain reducing CPM "rather than
  supporting the hypothesis that inefficient conditioned pain modulation
  mechanisms are leading to the development of neuropathic pain" (Gagné et
  al., PMID 33016208).
- **Sciatica.** In 111 patients, CPM measured six weeks after hospital
  discharge did not predict leg pain at 12 months: "crude Odds Ratio 0.87, 95%
  CI 0.7-1.1, p = 0.23" (Fjeld et al., Oslo, PMID 31527299).
- **General population.** In 1269 Danish adults followed for five years, CPM
  did not predict new functional somatic disorder: "OR = 0.96, 95% CI:
  0.90-1.03" (Gormsen et al., PMID 42400465). The outcome is not a pain
  diagnosis as such, and the authors list a 57.7% response rate and low event
  numbers as limits.
- **Musculoskeletal pain, pooled.** Across cohort studies and trials, baseline
  CPM correlated with follow-up pain, "mean r = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.20-0.50"
  (Georgopoulos et al., PMID 31045746). This is a pooled correlation
  coefficient. Squared, it is about 13% of variance, and that arithmetic is
  this brief's.

The spinal cord injury pair is the important one for causal direction. If pain
itself lowers CPM, a low CPM measured in someone who already has pain predicts
outcome only in the sense that having pain now predicts having pain later.

### 3.2 Response to treatment

#### The founding claim

> **Yarnitsky D, Granot M, Nahman-Averbuch H, Khamaisi M, Granovsky Y.
> "Conditioned pain modulation predicts duloxetine efficacy in painful diabetic
> neuropathy." *Pain* 2012;153(6):1193-1198. PMID 22480803.** Haifa.

30 patients with painful diabetic neuropathy, a placebo week followed by a
serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor, not randomised (PubMed type:
controlled clinical trial). "Baseline CPM was found to be correlated with
duloxetine efficacy (r=0.628, P<.001, efficient CPM is marked negative), such
that less efficient CPM predicted efficacious use of duloxetine." The
direction matters: **the people with the weaker brake did better on the drug
thought to strengthen it.** That is the attractive part of the idea, because
it would be a mechanism-matched prediction and not a general prognostic one.

The predictor reported is higher baseline pain ratings to tonic heat (r = 0.47; P = 0.013). **The abstract does not report CPM as a predictor.** The authors read the result as support for the same idea in a broader form: pronociceptive patients "seem to be more likely than non-pronociceptive ones to benefit" |
| Ferreira 2024, PMID 37994185 | Bauru, Brazil. No shared author | painful temporomandibular disorder; randomised, double-blind, same drug against placebo | 80 | The drug did not beat placebo (p = .82). "A more efficient CPM was associated with a greater pain intensity reduction regardless of the treatment group (p = .035)" |
| Petersen 2022, PMID 35638317 | Aalborg. No shared author | knee osteoarthritis; randomised double-blind crossover, same drug against placebo | 25 | No group-level difference from placebo. A model combining QST, cognitive factors and clinical pain predicted response; CPM alone is not reported as a predictor |
| Kreutzfeldt 2026, PMID 42956041 | Odense. No shared author | peripheral neuropathic pain; randomised double-blind crossover of a noradrenaline-dopamine reuptake inhibitor against placebo | 67 | Drug better than placebo by 0.5 points on a 0-10 scale. "No relation was found between response to treatment and efficiency of CPM or sensory phenotype" |
| Niesters 2014, PMID 24713310 | Leiden. No shared author | diabetic polyneuropathy; randomised, an opioid agonist that also inhibits noradrenaline reuptake, against placebo | 24 | Not a prediction study. CPM rose on the drug and "coincided with significant analgesic responses". No patient had significant CPM at baseline, so baseline CPM could not discriminate |
| Edwards 2016, PMID 27412526 | Boston | knee osteoarthritis; uncontrolled course of a topical anti-inflammatory | 35 | **Higher** baseline CPM, more pain reduction (p < .05) |
| Sendel 2022, PMID 34895019 | Kiel | mixed chronic pain; 10-day multimodal inpatient programme, uncontrolled | 224 recruited, 51% completed | Sufficient baseline CPM, "more pronounced reduction" in pain straight after treatment. "This was not the case 3 months after therapy" |
| Bruehl 2021, PMID 33086239 | Nashville / Chicago | 92 chronic low back pain and 99 healthy; opioid against placebo on **laboratory** pain | 191 | Greater CPM, "greater morphine analgesia" on experimental pain tasks |
| Olesen 2013, PMID 23469256 | Aalborg | painful chronic pancreatitis; gabapentinoid against placebo | 64 | A static electrical-threshold ratio predicted response. CPM was recorded and "No other parameters were predictive" |

The pattern is the finding.

**There are two different predictions in this table and they point in opposite
directions.** The founding claim is that a *weak* brake predicts benefit from a
drug that acts on the brake. Three later studies find that a *strong* brake
predicts doing better, on a topical anti-inflammatory, on an inpatient
programme, and on placebo as much as on the active drug (PMIDs 27412526,
34895019, 37994185). **Reading:** the second pattern is what a general marker
of good prognosis looks like. It does not depend on what the treatment is.

**The mechanism-matched version has not been independently reproduced.** No
study outside Haifa reporting "less efficient CPM predicts more benefit from a
reuptake inhibitor" was found by these queries (appendix Q14, Q15, Q18, Q20;
looser cross-check Q58). The same laboratory's own randomised follow-up of the same
drug, in migraine, reports a different predictor (baseline pain sensitivity,
not CPM), which its authors read as consistent with the broader idea that
more pronociceptive patients benefit more. The only independent randomised test
in neuropathic pain, with a drug of a neighbouring class, found no relation at
n=67. Its abstract describes the drug as one "that specifically inhibits
neuronal noradrenaline and dopamine reuptake", so this is a test of a
neighbouring mechanism and not a direct replication.

**One related experiment has an obvious rival explanation.** In 50 healthy
volunteers a gabapentinoid changed CPM most in those who started lowest,
"r = -0.73, p < 0.0001", with no such correlation on placebo (Sugimine et al.,
PMID 28169487). **Reading:** a negative correlation between a baseline and its
own change is what regression to the mean produces from an unreliable
measure (§2). The placebo comparison argues against that here, but the
measure's reliability means this needs a replication before it carries weight.

**Individual-level prediction from QST has been tested at scale in
osteoarthritis and failed.** An individual-participant-data meta-analysis of
2522 participants concludes that QST methods "do not consistently predict pain
or disability on an individual level in hip or knee osteoarthritis", at
very-low certainty for the few positive signals (Murphy et al.,
PMID 40310871). The abstract's positive signals are detection and tolerance
thresholds. It does not report a CPM signal.

**Grade for "less efficient CPM predicts benefit from a drug that enhances
descending inhibition": C.** One study of 30 from one group, not randomised,
not independently reproduced, with an adjacent independent null.

**Grade for "more efficient CPM predicts better outcome whatever the
treatment": B.** Three groups, three settings, same direction, but none
controlled for the ordinary prognostic factors that CPM is correlated with,
and one of the three effects had gone by three months.

### 3.3 Sensory profile as a predictor: the stronger precedent

The best-controlled evidence that a pre-treatment sensory test can predict
drug response uses static QST, not CPM, and it comes from one group.

- **Demant et al., *Pain* 2014, PMID 25139589.** Odense. Randomised,
  double-blind, placebo-controlled and **stratified by phenotype in advance**:
  97 randomised, 83 analysed, 31 with the "irritable nociceptor" profile
  (hypersensitivity with preserved small-fibre function) and 52 without. The
  treatment-by-phenotype interaction was "0.7 (95% CI 0.01-1.4, P=0.047)" on a
  0-10 scale. Numbers needed to treat for 50% relief were 3.9 (95% CI 2.3-12)
  with the profile and 13 (95% CI 5.3-∞) without.
- **Demant et al., *Pain* 2015, PMID 26090758.** Same group, same design, a
  topical local anaesthetic, 46 randomised. "there was no significant
  interaction between treatment and phenotype" on the primary measures. The
  authors attribute this to "too low statistical power".

The design is the right one: phenotype fixed before randomisation, interaction
as the primary test. The result is one interaction whose confidence interval
touches zero, and one null. Not found by these queries: a phenotype-stratified
trial of this kind from an independent group (appendix Q29; looser Q25).

## 4. CRPS and neuropathic pain specifically

### 4.1 CRPS: the brake is reported intact, twice, independently

The PubMed query for CPM or DNIC with CRPS returns seven records (appendix
Q22; looser cross-check Q73 returns 47, mostly reviews). Three contain
original CPM data in adults with CRPS.

> **Kumowski N, Hegelmaier T, Kolbenschlag J, Maier C, Mainka T, Vollert J,
> Enax-Krumova E. "Unimpaired endogenous pain inhibition in the early phase of
> complex regional pain syndrome." *Eur J Pain* 2017;21(5):855-865.
> PMID 28146319.** Bochum.

24 patients with CRPS of under one year's duration, 17 with neuralgia, 23
healthy. The CPM effect, in points on a 0-100 rating scale, was "healthy:
-12.5 ± 12.4 ... CRPS: -14.7 ± 15.7; neuralgia: -7.9 ± 9.8". So CPM in
CRPS was not reduced and was, if anything, larger: pain fell more steeply
during conditioning than in healthy subjects, only the CRPS group showed a
late CPM effect, and the authors write of the "unexpectedly high CPM in CRPS
patients". Conclusion: "An impairment
of endogenous pain inhibition does not explain the extent of pain in the early
stage of CRPS or in neuralgia."

A registry record from the same university, NCT02261012 (n=62, completed
2015), states its hypothesis in advance as "decreased conditioned pain
modulation" in CRPS. **Whether that record is the Kumowski study is
`[UNVERIFIED]`**; the sample sizes are close but the comparison group is
described differently. If it is, this is a pre-stated prediction that failed
and was published as a failure.

> **Allmendinger F, Sirucek L, De Schoenmacker I, Scheuren PS, Brunner F,
> Schweinhardt P, Hubli M. "Intact endogenous pain inhibition in complex
> regional pain syndrome type 1." *Pain Rep* 2025;10(6):e1358.
> PMID 41244943.** Zurich. No author in common with the Bochum paper.

16 patients with **chronic** CRPS type 1 and 15 matched controls, two CPM
designs, two body sites. "The CPM capacity did not differ between individuals
with CRPS and HC (P's > 0.05)", and CPM "did not correlate with individual
pain characteristics (ie, pain intensity, pain extent, and pain duration;
P's > 0.05)". The authors' inference: "pathomechanisms underlying chronic CRPS
are likely related to increased pain facilitatory rather than impaired
inhibitory processes."

The third dataset is 31 patients with CRPS tested before and after three
months of spinal cord stimulation in a Rotterdam trial: "A CPM effect was
present both before and after SCS" (Kriek et al., PMID 36050204).

What this does and does not show:

- Two groups, early and chronic disease, the same answer. For a field this
  small that is real convergence.
- The samples are 24, 16 and 31. A group mean that matches controls does not
  exclude a subgroup with poor inhibition. None of the three abstracts reports
  a subgroup analysis by allodynia.
- None of the three is a cohort of long-standing lower-limb CRPS with touch-
  evoked allodynia as the dominant feature. The abstracts do not give limb or
  allodynia status in enough detail to say more.
- Not found by these queries: any study testing whether CPM predicts the
  course of CRPS or its response to any treatment (appendix Q105, six records,
  none on point by title).

### 4.2 CRPS: the facilitation side

If inhibition is intact, the candidates left are facilitation and the dorsal
horn itself.

- **Meta-analysis of QST in CRPS** (Sobeeh et al., PMID 36593515; 37 adult
  studies, 14 low quality, 22 fair, 1 good). Adults with CRPS showed loss of
  thermal, mechanical and vibration detection, gain in thermal and mechanical
  pain thresholds, an "increased area of pinprick hyperalgesia", and "no
  difference in wind-up ratio".
- **Temporal summation and spread** (De Schoenmacker et al., Zurich,
  PMID 36946277; 21 patients, 20 controls). Temporal summation was increased in
  the affected area and "even more enhanced in patients with a high compared
  to a low spatial pain extent".
- **Before the disease starts** (Bruehl et al., PMID 35470089; 110 people
  having knee replacement, none with prior CRPS). Higher pre-operative
  temporal summation predicted CRPS severity scores at six weeks and six
  months. A CRPS *diagnosis* at six months "was predicted only by more
  widespread and intense pain preoperatively".
- **A spinal pharmacological probe** (Rauck, North & Eisenach, PMID 25599305;
  22 people with CRPS of a lower limb, double-blind crossover). An intrathecal
  α2-adrenoceptor agonist and intrathecal adenosine "similarly reduced areas of
  hyperalgesia and allodynia by approximately 30% and also inhibited temporal
  summation", but differed in their effect on pain, and "analgesia does not
  parallel antihyperalgesia with these treatments". This matters here because
  α2-adrenoceptors are where descending noradrenergic inhibition acts in the
  dorsal horn (§5.4). It is an acute laboratory result at two hours.

Wind-up is reported unchanged in the pooled analysis and increased in a single
later study. These are different measures in different samples and the brief
does not reconcile them. **Reading:** in CRPS the evidence for increased
facilitation is suggestive and inconsistent, and the evidence for reduced
inhibition is consistently negative.

### 4.3 Sensory-profile clustering

The DFNS network has built the largest datasets in this area, and the same
core authors appear across all of them.

- **1236 patients with neuropathic pain syndromes** (Maier et al.,
  PMID 20627413). 92% had at least one QST abnormality. "Every single sensory
  abnormality occurred in each neurological syndrome, but with different
  frequencies: thermal and mechanical hyperalgesias were most frequent in
  complex regional pain syndrome and peripheral nerve injury".
- **Three clusters in peripheral neuropathic pain** (Baron et al.,
  PMID 27893485; 902 patients, validated in 233). The clusters are "sensory
  loss, 42%", "thermal hyperalgesia, 33%" and "mechanical hyperalgesia, 24%". "All
  clusters occurred across etiologies but frequencies differed."
- **A sorting algorithm** (Vollert et al., PMID 28595241). It separates
  patients from healthy subjects with "sensitivity: 78%, specificity: 94%". In
  335 patients with painful peripheral nerve injury the profile frequencies
  were 37% sensory loss, 59% mechanical hyperalgesia and 50% thermal
  hyperalgesia; the abstract notes that these percentages overlap and do not
  add.
- **CRPS against nerve injury** (Gierthmühlen et al., PMID 22154921; upper-limb
  CRPS-I n=298, CRPS-II n=46, nerve injury n=72). "Patients with CRPS-I had
  more sensory gain (heat and pressure pain) and less sensory loss than
  patients with PNI". Dynamic mechanical allodynia was present in 24% to 28%
  of the CRPS groups.
- **Data-driven classification of CRPS itself** (Vollert et al., *Br J
  Anaesth* 2026, PMID 42538249). 604 patients, mean disease duration 1.6 years
  (SD 2.9), clustered in a training set of 380 and confirmed in 224. Three
  phenotypes: "pronounced hypersensitivity towards cold and heat pain (n=382)",
  "loss of thermal and mechanical sensation (n=200)", and "a third, small, but
  consistent, group [that] exhibited strong allodynia and mechanical
  hyperalgesia (n=22)".
- **What does not separate CRPS patients** (Sobeeh et al., PMID 37930043).
  Type I against type II, upper against lower limb, and male against female
  showed no significant QST differences. Warm against cold CRPS did.
- **Clusters cut across diagnoses** (De Schoenmacker et al., PMID 38027464; 20
  CRPS, 61 low back pain, 63 controls). Two clusters, neither matching a
  diagnosis. One showed widespread hypersensitivity; the other "showed a
  similar sensory profile as HC in both tested areas".

How this bears on descending modulation:

**The DFNS cluster analyses listed above do not include a measure of
descending inhibition.** The two DFNS analyses (PMIDs 27893485, 42538249) use
13 QST parameters from the DFNS protocol, and the abstract that defines that
protocol lists no CPM test (§1.4). The Zurich analysis describes its inputs as
QST measures at a painful and a pain-free site, and its abstract does not
mention CPM. Those profiles describe thresholds and wind-up. They say nothing,
either way, about the brake.

That statement is limited to those papers. **Cluster analyses that include
CPM do exist in other pain populations**, and an earlier version of this
brief was wrong to imply otherwise. A looser query, `(cluster analysis OR
latent class OR k-means OR hierarchical clustering OR clusters)[tiab] AND
"conditioned pain modulation"[tiab]`, returns 34 records (appendix Q112). All
34 titles were screened and seven abstracts were read:

- **Adolescents with neuropathic pain or CRPS** (Verriotis et al., London,
  PMID 33394878). 66 adolescents, 52 with neuropathic pain and 14 with CRPS.
  Individual QST plots were "matched to mechanism-related sensory profiles
  (sensory loss, thermal hyperalgesia, and mechanical hyperalgesia)", and CPM
  was measured in the same people: "Conditioned pain modulation was inhibitory
  in 54%, facilitatory in 14%". This is the one study found that puts the DFNS
  profiles and CPM in the same cohort that includes CRPS. The abstract does
  not report CPM by profile or separately for the 14 with CRPS. It is
  profile matching, not a cluster analysis, and it is in children.
- **Young people with chronic pain, clustered on CPM and temporal summation**
  (Ocay et al., Montreal, PMID 36213595; 668 participants aged 8 to 21). Three
  clusters; the third (n = 151) combined "presence of TSP during the test
  stimuli, and inefficient inhibitory CPM". The clusters account for "27.15%
  of the total variation in the data".
- **Young people with chronic back pain** (same group, PMID 35090183; n=198).
  CPM was among the inputs. The three clusters the abstract describes are
  defined by thresholds and psychosocial measures, and CPM is not named as a
  distinguishing feature.
- **Adults with chronic low back pain** (Rabey et al., Perth, PMID 26020225;
  n=294). CPM was among the inputs to a latent class analysis. The three
  clusters are described by temperature and pressure pain sensitivity;
  temporal summation differed between them, and the abstract does not name
  CPM as a distinguishing feature.
- **Adults with knee osteoarthritis** (Lee et al., Tucson, PMID 42605842;
  n=208). Five clusters, two of them labelled by impaired CPM ("high pressure
  pain sensitivity and impaired descending inhibition", n = 86; "high pain
  sensitivity and impaired pain modulation", n = 26).
- **Early knee osteoarthritis** (Neelapala et al., PMID 37709187; n=750).
  Latent classes built from inputs including CPM; "none of the phenotypes in
  either model had a significant relationship with pain worsening" at two
  years.
- **Young adults with low back pain** (Lyons et al., PMID 41001699; n=78).
  Three clusters from five variables including CPM.

**Reading:** when CPM is put into a cluster analysis it sometimes defines a
subgroup (young people with chronic pain, knee osteoarthritis) and sometimes
does not (the two back pain cohorts). None of these is an adult CRPS or adult
neuropathic pain cohort. Not found by these queries: a cluster analysis in
adults with CRPS or peripheral neuropathic pain that includes CPM, or a study
reporting CPM by DFNS cluster (appendix Q26, Q30, Q112).

A disclosure about method. Q30 returned 24 records and was screened by title
only. PMID 33394878 was in it and was missed, although its title names both
somatosensory profiles and conditioned pain modulation. The same record is
also in Q22 and Q23.

**The allodynia-dominant CRPS phenotype is the rare one.** 22 of 604 is 3.6%;
that division is this brief's. **Reading:** if touch-evoked allodynia is the
defining feature of a patient group, the published CRPS cohorts contain very
few such people, and group-level findings about "CRPS" (including the two
intact-CPM studies in §4.1) may not describe them. With a mean disease
duration of 1.6 years, that cohort also says little about long-standing
disease.

### 4.4 Neuropathic pain: no consistent deficit

The 2012 meta-analysis in §2.5 found reduced CPM across chronic pain in
general. Within neuropathic pain the studies do not line up.

| Study | Group | Comparison | n | CPM finding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nahman-Averbuch 2011, PMID 21458224 | Haifa | painful against painless chemotherapy-induced polyneuropathy | 27 (12 painful) | **Less efficient** in the painful group (P = 0.03) |
| Granovsky 2022, PMID 34371518 | Haifa, with Oxford and London co-authors | painful against painless diabetic polyneuropathy | 271 | **More efficient** in the painful group for heat (-7.4 ± 1.0 against -2.3 ± 1.6; P = 0.008); no difference for pressure |
| Granovsky 2017, PMID 29392208 | Haifa | painful diabetic neuropathy, by pain duration | 33 | Longer duration, **more efficient** CPM (ρ = -0.417; P = 0.025); over two years, no different from healthy controls |
| Miclescu 2021, PMID 33688603 | Uppsala | painful against painless neuropathy **after surgical repair of traumatic nerve injury** | 131 (69 painful) | **No difference** (P = 0.19). Recovery after the conditioning stimulus was slower with pain: 42 against 28 seconds |
| Bubenzer 2026, PMID 42004429 | Bochum | polyneuropathy against healthy | 44 and 19 | "The CPM-effects were similar between groups" |
| Gruener 2016, PMID 26894913 | Tel Aviv | spinal cord injury with and without central neuropathic pain, and healthy | 27, 23, 20 | **Decreased** with central neuropathic pain |
| Lütolf 2022, PMID 36000307 | Zurich | spinal cord injury with neuropathic pain against healthy | 17 and 17 | **No group difference**; with a sham-controlled design neither group showed significant inhibition |

Three observations.

**The largest study contradicts the expected direction, and its own authors
explain why.** In 271 people with diabetic polyneuropathy the painful group
had *better* CPM, which the authors attribute partly to "altered sensory
messages coming from tested affected body sites", adding that this "calls for
the use of intact sites for proper assessment of pain modulation in patients
with neuropathy" (PMID 34371518). **Reading:** in anyone with sensory loss at
the test site, a CPM number is partly a measure of the neuropathy.

**In the group closest to post-surgical nerve injury, CPM did not separate
painful from painless.** The Uppsala study is the only one found in people
after operative nerve repair. It is in the upper limb, and its positive
finding, slower recovery after a painful stimulus, is a single unreplicated
result.

**A sham-controlled CPM design removed the effect in healthy people too.** The
Zurich spinal cord injury study used a lukewarm bath as a sham conditioning
stimulus and then found no significant inhibition in either group
(PMID 36000307). That is one study with 17 per group. It is also the kind of
control §2.3 argues for, and it is a reason to be cautious about every
uncontrolled CPM figure in this brief.

## 5. What is reported to change descending modulation

Everything in this section is a report of what a study observed. None of it is
a recommendation, and no doses are given. In most of these studies the outcome
is the CPM number, with all the limits of §2.

### 5.1 Exercise

- **A single bout, healthy people.** A meta-analysis of randomised studies
  found that "aerobic exercise caused large EIH (7 studies, 236 participants;
  g = -.85 [-1.58, -.13])", where EIH is exercise-induced hypoalgesia, a drop
  in sensitivity to experimental pain. Isometric exercise did not
  (Wewege & Jones, PMID 32599154). The authors call the data "low-quality"
  and from "small samples". This is a standardised effect on laboratory pain
  thresholds. It is not a CPM result.
- **A single bout, chronic musculoskeletal pain.** Same review: isometric
  exercise "did not cause EIH" (3 studies, 114 participants; g = -.41, with a
  confidence interval from -1.08 to .25), and there were no eligible aerobic
  studies.
- **Training programmes, chronic musculoskeletal pain.** A meta-analysis of 13
  studies: "We did not find an overall effect of physical exercise on TSP and
  CPM", with a favourable subgroup signal for motor control exercise in chronic
  neck pain (Chen et al., PMID 38662515).
- **Patients can respond in the opposite direction.** In a randomised crossover
  in rheumatoid arthritis, chronic fatigue with fibromyalgia, and healthy
  controls (n=53), "CPM responses to exercise are inconclusive, but seem to
  worsen after exercise" (Meeus et al., PMID 24528544).

Not found by these queries: a trial of exercise with CPM as an outcome in CRPS
or in peripheral neuropathic pain (appendix Q47, 26 records, none in those
populations by title).

### 5.2 Sleep

- **The pooled answer is null overall and positive in women.** A systematic
  review of 37 studies found that experimental sleep disturbance did not
  significantly change CPM in healthy people overall ("CPM: .40 [95%CI: -.06
  to .85] P = .088"), and that in clinical populations sleep and CPM were not
  significantly correlated. Where results were reported by sex, "experimental
  sleep disturbances impaired pain inhibition in females (1.43 [95%CI:
  .98-1.88]; P < .001) but not in males" (Herrero Babiloni et al.,
  PMID 37914093).
- **The classic positive.** In 32 healthy women, forced awakenings through the
  night produced "a significant loss of pain inhibition and an increase in
  spontaneous pain", while the same amount of sleep lost by a later bedtime
  did not. The authors' reading is "that sleep continuity disturbance, but not
  simple sleep restriction, impairs endogenous pain-inhibitory function"
  (Smith et al., PMID 17520794). The groups were 12, 10 and 10.
- **A positive after total deprivation.** One night without sleep in 24
  healthy participants: "Conditioned pain modulation was significantly
  impaired, temporal summation was significantly facilitated" (Staffe et al.,
  PMID 31800612). No control night in a separate group.
- **A clean contradiction.** After two nights of half-length sleep in 22
  healthy people, heat was rated more painful, and yet "A stronger inhibitory
  CPM was found after SR versus after HS (p < 0.001)" (Matre et al.,
  PMID 26104968).
- **Time of day.** In 48 healthy men tested morning, afternoon and evening,
  thermal pain measures varied and there were "no significant diurnal
  differences in the mechanical evoked pain parameters or in either of the
  'dynamic' pain paradigms", CPM included (Aviram, Shochat & Pud,
  PMID 25545856). A study of 18 volunteers reported a genotype-dependent
  change in CPM across the day (PMID 31249322). At n=18, split by genotype,
  that is not evidence of anything yet.

**Reading:** disrupted sleep raises pain sensitivity fairly reliably. Whether
it does so *through* descending inhibition is not settled, and the one study
that separated fragmentation from restriction has 10 people per arm. This
bears on PB-3 (§8.1).

### 5.3 Expectation and placebo

This is where the measurement problem and the mechanism become the same thing.

- **Placebo analgesia uses the descending system.** In a functional imaging
  study, an opioid antagonist "reduced both behavioral and neural placebo
  effects" and "abolished placebo-induced coupling between rACC and PAG, which
  predicted both neural and behavioral placebo effects as well as activation
  of the RVM" (Eippert et al., *Neuron* 2009, PMID 19709634). This is the
  strongest single piece of human evidence that expectation acts through the
  brainstem circuit of §1.1.
- **Expectation changes the CPM number.** In 88 people with chronic low back
  pain and 100 controls, those who expected the conditioning stimulus to lower
  their pain "reported significantly larger decreases in heat pain" than those
  who expected no change or an increase, and this was unaffected by an opioid
  agonist or antagonist. The authors' warning: "significant caution is needed
  when interpreting findings that do not account for this individual
  difference" (France et al., PMID 26809850).
- **A suggestion can convert non-inhibitors.** In 43 healthy participants, an
  audiovisual suggestion of analgesia reduced the proportion classed as
  non-inhibitors from "51.2%-72.1%" at baseline to "32.6%-44.2%". The abstract
  gives these as ranges, apparently across the test stimuli and sites used
  (Nascimento et al., PMID 39552267).
- **It does not always.** In a randomised crossover of 72 healthy adults,
  attention directed at the conditioning stimulus reduced CPM, and neither
  distraction "nor did a priori expectations" change it (Billens et al.,
  PMID 40542602). The same group's meta-analysis of 24 articles concludes that
  "expectations and attentional focus appear to influence CPM" on evidence
  that is "limited and conflicting" (PMID 39541722).
- **CPM and placebo response are not the same trait.** In 19 patients with
  neuropathic pain, "The CPM response did not predict the magnitude of the
  placebo effect (p = 0.765)" (Skyt et al., PMID 29266627). In 273 pain-free
  people, different psychological factors were associated with being a
  responder to each (PMID 40692375).

**Reading:** two consequences follow, and they pull in different directions.
First, any open-label treatment that a person expects to help can be expected
to engage this system, which is one mechanism for the gap between blinded and
unblinded results that E-03 documents for spinal cord stimulation. Second, a
CPM value measured in an unblinded setting is contaminated by the same
expectation, so "treatment X improved CPM" from an uncontrolled before-and-
after study cannot distinguish a drug or device effect from an expectation
effect. Most of §6.1 is exactly that design.

| α2-adrenoceptor agonist, oral | "no effect was observed on pain perception, pain modulation, and psychological parameters" | 40 healthy, randomised double-blind | PMID 26893118 |
| α2-adrenoceptor antagonists (rat) | "markedly attenuated" DNIC | anaesthetised rats | PMID 26010460 |
| 5-HT3 antagonist; noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor (rat) | DNIC, "abolished after neuropathy", was restored | rats after spinal nerve ligation | PMID 26010460 |

Four points.

**The placebo row is the most informative one in the table.** In ten patients
with neuropathic pain and no measurable CPM, an inactive infusion produced a
CPM response that was not statistically different from that of two active
drugs (PMID 23384733). The
abstract reads this as "a role for CPM engagement in analgesic efficacy of
ketamine, morphine, and placebo treatment". **Reading:** it is equally a
demonstration that the act of being treated moves the measure, which is §5.3
again.

**The animal mechanism is specific and the human evidence for it is not.** In
rats, DNIC depends on spinal α2-adrenoceptors, is lost after nerve injury, and
returns when descending serotonergic facilitation is blocked or noradrenergic
tone is raised (Bannister et al., PMID 26010460). Mouse work from a Montreal
laboratory, with a co-author from that first group, reaches a compatible
conclusion and adds that with weak test stimuli the
same paradigm produces hyperalgesia, which reuptake inhibitors abolished when
given spinally "but not systemically" (Nemoto et al., PMID 36448969). In
humans, the two α2-agonist studies above disagree with each other, and one of
them reports the agonist *reducing* CPM. The abstract of that study notes this
is "consistent with results from animal studies" of systemic administration.
Route of administration appears to matter, and the human data do not resolve
it.

**The reuptake-inhibitor rows repeat §3.2.** One uncontrolled positive in 30
people, one randomised null in 25.

**Opioids point both ways depending on timescale and design.** Acutely they are
reported to reduce CPM, leave it unchanged, or enhance experimental analgesia
in those with good CPM (§3.2). The long-term finding is cross-sectional: people
on opioids may have lower CPM because of the drug, or may be on opioids
because they are a group with worse pain.

Three separate questions, with three different answers. No stimulation

### 6.1 Does stimulation change CPM or QST?

| Kemler 2001, PMID 11465587 | Maastricht | SCS with physical therapy against physical therapy alone, randomised, 12 months | 54 with CRPS type I | not tested | "no long-term effect on detection and pain thresholds"; mechanical hyperalgesia "reduced slightly" |

What the table supports:

- **For SCS, six studies in the table report on CPM or on QST that included
  it, and they split three ways.** Three small unblinded studies report better
  CPM with stimulation (Mannheim, n=8; Bochum, n=28 of whom 15 had SCS;
  London, n=23). One is mixed: Brussels (n=17) found no difference in CPM
  responders on the symptomatic side and more on the other side. Two report
  no change: Rotterdam (n=31, CRPS, from a baseline where CPM was already
  present) and Baltimore ("few changes in QST over time"). Every one of the
  positive three is a before-and-after or on-and-off comparison in people who
  knew their device state. By §5.3 that design cannot separate a stimulation
  effect from an expectation effect. The only blinded on-off comparison in the
  table did not measure CPM, and found no change in static thresholds.
- **For DRG stimulation, three studies measured CPM and none reports a
  change.** Two are mixed cohorts of SCS and DRG stimulation from one Ann
  Arbor laboratory: 16 patients with "no effects" on CPM, and 33 enrolled (18
  followed) in whom QST results "typically failed to reach statistical
  significance". Whether the two cohorts overlap is not stated in the
  abstracts. The third is 11 patients with DRG stimulation alone. All three
  are pilots, and neither mixed-cohort abstract gives a result for the DRG
  patients separately.
- **The more consistent signal is on the facilitation side.** Reduced temporal
  summation or wind-up is reported with SCS in Mannheim, with mixed SCS and
  DRG stimulation in Ann Arbor, and with DRG stimulation in CRPS in Erlangen,
  where it tracked pain relief. **Reading:** if implanted stimulation moves a
  measurable central process, the evidence points at spinal summation more
  than at the descending brake. That fits §4.1 and §4.2.
- The second Bochum result is another correlation between a baseline value and
  its own change, with the same regression-to-the-mean caution as in §3.2.

A systematic review of proposed supraspinal mechanisms of SCS and DRG
stimulation found 69 hypotheses in 54 publications, of which "descending
pathways (n = 24)" was the largest category (Goudman et al., PMID 34201877).
The animal basis is real: in a rat mononeuropathy model, spinal serotonin
content rose in animals that responded to SCS and not in those that did not
(Song et al., PMID 19836134), and a systematic review of the rodent literature
concludes that SCS "restores the inhibitory function of the descending
serotonergic system" (Heijmans, Mons & Joosten, PMID 34662215). That is rodent
evidence about SCS. Not found by these queries: equivalent animal or human
work for DRG stimulation (appendix Q38, restricted to SCS by construction;
Q36 and Q91 for DRG).

### 6.2 Does baseline CPM or QST predict response to spinal cord stimulation?

Three studies, and they disagree on direction.

| Study | Group | n | Outcome | Baseline CPM in those who did well |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Campbell 2015, PMID 25800088 | Baltimore | not given in the abstract | clinical pain 3 months after implant | **Reduced.** "enhanced central sensitization and reduced CPM were associated with less self-reported pain 3 months following SCS implantation" |
| Ramaswamy 2019, PMID 30303595 | London | 23 | "successful implant" at 3 months | **Inefficient.** "All the patients who had an inefficient CPM at baseline had a successful implant"; "Only 50% of the patients with an efficient CPM at baseline, had a successful implant" |
| El-Sayed 2026, PMID 41937366 | Toronto | 40 | at least 30% pain reduction after a 12-day trial | **Efficient.** "Nonresponders exhibited significantly abnormal inefficient CPM" |

A systematic review with a search run on 4 June 2026 covers this ground and
reaches the same verdict: twelve studies, of which "QST and CPM findings were
promising but directionally inconsistent. All 12 studies were at high overall
risk of bias" (Odonkor et al., *J Pers Med* 2026, PMID 42930761). It reports
that neither of the two multivariable prediction models in the field "underwent
external validation".

An older pilot that enrolled 13 patients, with usable data from 12, found that vibration threshold and tolerance to
electrical stimulation changed during an SCS trial and that these changes
"correlated with the decision regarding the permanent implantation"
(Eisenberg et al., PMID 17147592). That is a change during the trial, not a
baseline predictor.

Three further limits:

- **None of the three outcomes is durability.** Two are at three months, one
  is at twelve days.
- **The two that agree are the two earliest.** One gives no sample size in its
  abstract. The other has 23 patients, and its authors call their own numbers
  "very low".
- **The direction that the first two report is the mechanism-matched one of
  §3.2**: a weak brake predicts benefit from a treatment thought to engage the
  brake. The direction the most recent study reports, with the largest stated
  sample, is the general-prognosis one.
  The same split as in the drug literature has reappeared in the device
  literature.

## 7. Genetics, as a research question only

**The question.** Common genetic variants associated with chronic pain mostly
lie outside genes and are presumed to act by changing how much of a gene is
made, in particular cell types. Do they act in the cells of the descending
modulation system, or in sensory neurons, or elsewhere? An answer would say
where in the pathway inherited risk sits. It would say nothing about any
individual.

### 7.1 What genome-wide association work says about tissue and cell type

| Study | Sample and phenotype | What the abstract reports on tissue or cell type |
|---|---|---|
| Johnston et al., *PLoS Genet* 2019, PMID 31194737 | about 380,000 UK Biobank participants; number of chronic pain sites | 76 lead variants at 39 loci; SNP heritability 10.2%. Gene-level enrichment for "neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, nervous system development, cell-cycle progression and apoptosis". No tissue named |
| Johnston et al., *PLoS Genet* 2021, PMID 33830993 | the same phenotype, split by sex | "All 37 male MCP-associated genes and all but one of 31 female MCP-associated genes were found to be expressed in the dorsal root ganglion". This is expression, which is weaker than enrichment. The authors read the whole as "a strong central nervous-system component ... additionally highlighting a potential role for the DRG and nociception" |
| Toikumo et al., *Nat Med* 2024, PMID 38429522 | 598,339 US veterans, multi-ancestry; pain intensity | 126 loci. "enrichment for putatively causal genes (n = 142) and proteins (n = 14) expressed in brain tissues, specifically in GABAergic neurons" |
| Toikumo et al., *J Clin Invest* 2025, PMID 41055971 | chronic pain association data combined with single-cell data from human brain and human ganglia, and chromatin accessibility from human brain and mouse dorsal horn | Variants "were enriched in glutamatergic neurons, mainly in the prefrontal cortex, hippocampal CA1-3, and amygdala. In hDRG, the hPEP.TRPV1/A1.2 neuronal subtype showed robust enrichment." Accessibility analysis added neocortical neurons and, in mouse dorsal horn, "midventral neurons and oligodendrocyte precursor cells" |
| Kupari et al., *Nat Commun* 2021, PMID 33686078 | pain association data mapped to non-human primate ganglion neuron types | "Genome-wide associations for chronic pain converge on two different neuronal types" in the ganglion |

For neuropathic pain as such, the association studies are too small to
support a cell-type analysis. A deeply phenotyped Norwegian cohort of 1146
found "No significant associations" at genome-wide level (PMID 41359007). The
largest study, 33,278 cases defined from records and prescriptions, found
seven new associations and notes that before it "only 3 genome-wide
significant signals" existed (PMID 40899795). A cohort study with QST
reported one variant that differed between the "irritable" and "nonirritable"
sensory profiles of §3.3 (PMID 39471050); that is a single association
awaiting replication. For CRPS, a study of 230 patients and 230 controls
"did not find evidence that any of the investigated common SNPs may be
associated with CRPS phenotype" (PMID 28025368), which at that size excludes
only large effects.

For CPM itself there is no genome-wide study. `"conditioned pain
modulation"[tiab] AND ("genome-wide"[tiab] OR GWAS[tiab])` returns 0 (appendix
Q101; looser Q102 returns 4, none on point). Candidate-gene work has been
reviewed systematically: 24 articles, with "low-quality evidence" of *no*
association between CPM and the commonly studied catecholamine and serotonin
transporter variants, and the conclusion that "dynamic experimental pain
measurements are robust to genetic and epigenetic variations"
(Billens et al., PMID 40539463). Given §2, a trait measured this unreliably
would be expected to show weak genetic associations whatever the truth.

### 7.2 So: descending-modulation cells, or sensory neurons?

What the published enrichment results name is **forebrain neurons, one
nociceptor subtype in the ganglion, and cells of the dorsal horn.** That is
support for both a central and a peripheral sensory contribution to inherited
risk, with the larger share of the signal described in cortex and limbic
structures.

**No abstract read for this brief names a brainstem modulatory nucleus** (the
periaqueductal grey, the rostral ventromedial medulla, the locus coeruleus)
as enriched or as tested. Queries for association work naming these
structures returned nothing in PubMed (appendix Q64: 0; Q103: 0). A looser
full-text search in Europe PMC returned 73 records (Q104), of which the first
15 were screened by title; the only directly relevant one is the 2025 paper in
the table.

This is **not** evidence that pain-associated variants are absent from
descending-modulation cells. An enrichment analysis can only find cell types
that are in its reference atlas. **Whether the brain atlases used in
PMIDs 38429522 and 41055971 include brainstem nuclei at usable resolution is
`[UNVERIFIED]`**; it needs the full texts and supplements, which were not
read. If those nuclei were absent from the reference data, the question in the
heading has not been asked yet.

**Reading:** two things can be said. The forebrain is where the "top-down"
inputs to the circuit of §1.1 come from, and the placebo result in §5.3 shows
a cortical region driving the brainstem stations. So variants acting in
forebrain neurons could influence descending modulation from above without
acting in its own cells. And the peripheral signal is in a
nociceptor subtype, which keeps a sensory-neuron contribution alive. Neither
is specific enough to act on.

### 7.3 A tool that might help prioritise variants

AlphaGenome is a sequence-to-function model from Google DeepMind. The
peer-reviewed description says it "takes as input 1 Mb of DNA sequence and
predicts thousands of functional genomic tracks up to single-base-pair
resolution", and "matches or exceeds the strongest available external models
in 25 of 26 evaluations of variant effect prediction" (Avsec et al., *Nature*
2026, PMID 41606153). A preprint from the same company describes genome-wide
precomputed scores (PMID 42779988, medRxiv, not peer reviewed).

AlphaGenome 2 was announced on a company blog on 11 November 2026:
https://deepmind.google/blog/alphagenome-2-deciphering-how-cells-read-dna-to-better-understand-disease/

How to weigh that page:

- **It is a company claim.** The page links to a paper, which this brief did
  not read and whose review status it did not check. `"AlphaGenome 2"` returns
  one record in Europe PMC, and that record is a different model's paper
  (appendix Q70).
- **It is non-clinical, by its own statement.** Verbatim: "AlphaGenome and
  AlphaGenome 2 are intended for research use only and have not been validated
  or approved for clinical or diagnostic purposes."
- **It contains no pain content.** In the page text as retrieved on 18
  November 2026, the strings "pain", "nocicept", "neuropath", "dorsal root",
  "ganglion", "sensory neuron", "spinal" and "brainstem" each occur zero
  times. Its worked examples are mostly immune and blood cells. `AlphaGenome[tiab]
  AND pain[tiab]` returns 0 in PubMed (appendix Q69).
- **The claims relevant to §7.2**, quoted as claims: that the company
  "precomputed AlphaGenome 2 predictions for trait-associated variants across
  hundreds of human cell and tissue conditions", and that the model "can also
  generalize to entirely new single-cell experiments without needing to be
  retrained".

If the second claim holds, it is the capability the question in §7.2 lacks: a
way to ask what a pain-associated variant is predicted to do in a cell type
that the large atlases do not cover, given a small single-cell dataset from
that cell type. Human single-cell data exist for the ganglion
(PMID 41055971). Whether they exist for the brainstem modulatory nuclei was
not searched for this brief.

The limits are plain. The output would be a ranked list of computational
predictions about regulatory effect, each needing experimental confirmation.
It is not a test, has no bearing on any individual, and whether the model's
training or precomputed set covers any pain-relevant cell type is not stated
on the page.

### 8.1 Bearing on the hard core and the belt

| | Commitment | Bearing of this brief |
|---|---|---|
| HC-1 | Structure beyond mean rate | **Indirect.** Descending control acts on the convergent dorsal horn neurons and depresses their responses to both C-fibre and low-threshold input (PMID 460935). The same afferent pattern therefore produces a different spinal output depending on a state that is not in the afferent traffic. Any test of whether a pattern carries a percept needs that state held constant or measured. This does not touch the HC-1 killer |
| HC-2 | Readable outside the CNS | **Presses in the same direction as E-01 §6, with a caveat.** Descending modulation is a second way, besides dorsal horn disinhibition, for the pain-relevant discrimination to be made centrally. It needs supraspinal structures (PMID 460936), so no peripheral sensor can see it. The caveat is §4.1: in CRPS the inhibitory arm is reported intact in two independent cohorts, both small (n=24 and n=16), and in neuropathic pain the findings conflict (§4.4). So this is a mechanism that *could* defeat peripheral readout, without evidence that it is the operative one in the motivating phenotype |
| HC-3 | Structure-targeted beats channel destruction | **A confound more than a rival.** Expectation acts through the descending system (PMID 19709634) and moves the CPM measure (PMID 26809850). Any unblinded comparison of two interventions, including the head-to-head that the HC-3 killer describes, will contain an expectation effect delivered by this route. That reinforces E-03's weight on blinding. Separately, where implanted stimulation changes a central measure, the more consistent one is temporal summation and not CPM (§6.1) |
| HC-4 | A realisable transducer exists | **No bearing.** |
| PB-2 | Fading benefit is habituation to a fixed stimulus | **A rival explanation, with no test of it found by these queries.** A slow change in central state, either less inhibition or more facilitation, would also produce fading benefit, and would not respond to varying the stimulus. No study relating any descending-modulation measure to fading was found (§6.4), so this neither supports nor weakens PB-2. It adds an alternative PB-2 has to be distinguished from |
| PB-3 | Night-time allodynia has a distinct signature | **Thin, and it cuts against "distinct peripheral signature".** Fragmented sleep reduced pain inhibition and raised spontaneous pain in healthy women (PMID 17520794, 10 per arm), which offers a central route by which nights could be worse. Against that, CPM did not vary by time of day in 48 healthy men (PMID 25545856), and the pooled sleep effect is null outside women (PMID 37914093). Not found by these queries: CPM measured at night, or in relation to nocturnal allodynia, in any patient group (appendix Q109) |

**Reading, on the owner's question.** The pain-modulation system is not
ruled in or out as "a key". What the evidence does not support is the simple
version, a broken brake that explains CRPS pain and could be repaired. The two
studies that looked found the brake working, in 24 and 16 patients.

### 8.2 Bearing on the S-2 and S-2b dorsal-horn simulations

Source for the simulation facts: `ledger/OPEN.md` ("Simulations") and
`simulations/S-002-dorsal-horn/PREDICTION.md`. Nothing in the simulations was
changed for this brief.

1. **The model has no descending input, and its declared limits do not say
   so.** The pre-registration lists four cells: two substantia gelatinosa
   interneurons (cells 75 and 76, releasing GABA and glycine), one
   **excitatory** interneuron (cell 77, the "EX cell") and the
   wide-dynamic-range projection neuron (cell 78). Its inputs are Aβ, Aδ and C
   afferents and a surround input. The summary line in `ledger/OPEN.md` calls
   the third cell "an inhibitory interneuron"; the pre-registration is the
   source followed here, and the ledger was not edited. The word "descending"
   does not occur in the S-2 directory. The founding DNIC paper is specifically about that
   class of projection neuron: 67 of 68 convergent neurons were inhibited, and
   the companion paper found the effect absent in other dorsal horn cell types
   (PMIDs 460935, 460936). So the cell whose output S-2 scores is the cell
   that descending control is known to act on.
2. **S-2 question 1 is conditional on zero descending tone.** The question is
   at what anion shift touch starts to drive the projection neuron. The pilot
   onset reported in OPEN.md, between 0 and 10 mV in the unmodified model, is
   an onset with no descending inhibition and no descending facilitation. A
   tonic inhibitory input to the projection neuron would be expected to move
   that onset later, and a facilitatory one earlier. By how much is unknown,
   and there is no human measurement to set it.
3. **Which way the omission biases the model is itself contested.** In rats
   after nerve injury DNIC is "abolished" (PMID 26010460), which would make a
   model without descending inhibition *closer* to the neuropathic state. In
   people with CRPS, CPM is reported intact (§4.1), which would make it
   further away. The rat result is a neuronal recording under anaesthesia and
   the human result is a rating difference, so they are not measurements of the
   same thing.
4. **S-2 question 3 has an unmodelled fourth cause, and it is a specificity
   problem.** The pre-registration separates three causes of fading benefit:
   progressive disinhibition (a further anion shift), habituation (loss of
   stimulation efficacy), and lead drift. A fall in descending inhibition, or a
   rise in descending facilitation, is a fourth. It is a change in the circuit
   and not in the stimulus, so it may produce the same signature the model
   assigns to progressive disinhibition (selective loss of relief for
   touch-evoked output, and a moved optimum). If it does, that signature
   cannot tell local disinhibition from a change in descending control. That is
   the question the specificity column in OPEN.md says to ask before filing:
   can the measurement tell the difference?
5. **A pointer for after S-2b, not a change to it.** S-2b is defined by its
   intent file as a corrected control, a corrected stimulation driver and a
   corrected C-input grid. A descending-tone term is outside that scope. If a
   later run is ever pre-registered, a single tonic conductance on the
   projection neuron, varied across the ensemble, would be the smallest way to
   ask whether the P3 signature survives. Until then, any S-2b result would be
   most accurately stated as holding "with descending input absent".
6. **What the simulation line can take from §6.1.** The measure that most
   consistently moves with stimulation in patients is temporal summation, and
   the starting model's positive control includes wind-up, which passed in
   S-2. That is the one place where the model's output and a human measurement
   used in stimulation studies are the same kind of quantity.

### 8.3 A candidate conjecture, checked and not filed

> **Candidate.** Baseline descending modulation predicts durability of
> ganglion-stimulation benefit.

This is **not filed**. It is checked here against the specificity column in
`ledger/OPEN.md`, which records that four consecutive conjectures asked
whether something is true when they needed to ask whether the measurement
could tell the difference.

**As worded, it fails that check, for five reasons.**

1. **The measurement cannot carry the claim.** "Baseline descending
   modulation" can only mean a CPM test. Its between-session reliability in
   patients is 0.44 (95% CI 0.11-0.69) on the one protocol with data
   (PMID 35142147), and day-to-day agreement on who is a responder is poor
   (PMIDs 30007061, 31715262). The point estimate implies that a
   single baseline reading carries a large share of measurement error at the
   level of the individual. The interval is wide: it runs up to 0.69, which
   would be usable, and down to 0.11, which would not. So reliability in this
   population is poorly known and not shown to be adequate. A null result
   would not show that descending modulation is irrelevant, only that one
   reading of uncertain reliability did not predict.
2. **A positive result would not be specific to descending modulation.** CPM
   moves with expectation, attention, sleep, age, sex and opioid use (§2.3,
   §5). Each of those could plausibly predict durability on its own, and
   long-term opioid use already predicts poorer response to ganglion
   stimulation (PMID 33508161). An association between baseline CPM and
   durability would not separate "the brake matters" from "people with better
   general prognosis score better on CPM". This is the same defect that killed
   C-009: the design removes or measures both rivals together.
3. **A positive result would not be specific to ganglion stimulation.** More
   efficient CPM has predicted better outcome on a topical anti-inflammatory,
   on an inpatient programme, and on placebo as much as on active drug
   (§3.2). If CPM predicts doing well on anything, it says nothing about this
   therapy.
4. **The direction is not fixed, so the claim forbids nothing.** The SCS
   studies disagree on direction (§6.2). The mechanism-matched argument
   predicts that a *weak* brake goes with more benefit; the general-prognosis
   argument predicts the opposite. A conjecture that would count either
   outcome as "predicts" has no killer, and `tools/lint-conjecture.py` exists
   to reject exactly that.
5. **The target population may have no variance to predict from.** If CPM is
   intact in CRPS at group level (§4.1), baseline values may be bunched in the
   normal range, which lowers power further.

**What a version that could be refuted would have to state.** These are the
elements a test would need, set out for a researcher or trialist. They are not
a protocol.

- A direction, in advance.
- Baseline measured more than once, with a sequential protocol and a
  no-conditioning control session (§2.3), so that reliability is measured in
  the study and not assumed.
- Expectation of benefit, sleep, opioid use, pain duration and baseline pain
  intensity recorded as rival predictors.
- A durability outcome defined in advance and separated from hardware causes:
  for example, time to loss of a pre-defined level of relief, with lead
  migration and device failure counted apart.
- **A comparator that makes specificity testable**: the same baseline measure
  in people receiving a different therapy, or the same people's outcome on a
  different measure. The claim that is specific to ganglion stimulation is a
  *difference* in predictive value between arms.

**What would refute it.** With those elements in place: a confidence interval
on the association that excludes a pre-stated minimum effect in the pre-stated
direction; or an association of equal size in the comparator arm, which would
refute the therapy-specific claim and leave a general prognostic marker; or an
association that disappears once the rival predictors are in the model, which
would refute the claim that descending modulation is what carries it.

**Could existing data test it? Not found by these queries.**

- No study relating baseline CPM or QST to durability of any implanted
  stimulation was found (§6.4, seven query rows and one registry scan).
- The published datasets that hold a baseline CPM value before ganglion
  stimulation are three pilots: 16 patients mixed with SCS and measured once
  after treatment (PMID 33471409); from the same laboratory, 33 enrolled with
  18 followed and
  "appreciable attrition" to twelve months (PMID 40909374); and 11 patients
  measured at about one month (PMID 33369117). None is large enough or long
  enough, and only the smallest is restricted to ganglion stimulation.
- The large datasets with long follow-up did not register a sensory-testing
  outcome: the randomised trial of 152, the post-approval study of 426 and the
  recruiting real-world study with a target of 2000 (registry scan R1, §6.4).
  Whether any site collected CPM outside the registered outcomes is
  `[UNVERIFIED]`.

**Reading:** the conjecture as worded would be returned by triage as the
wrong question, for the reason the ledger already predicts. The question
underneath it that *is* askable is narrower: **in people with an implanted
ganglion stimulator, does a change in a dynamic sensory measure precede or
accompany fading benefit?** That is a within-person, repeated-measures
question. It does not need a reliable single baseline, it uses temporal
summation as readily as CPM, and it bears directly on S-2 question 3. No
data on it were found either.

### 8.4 Questions a treating team might consider

Framed as questions, for people who are qualified to answer them in a
particular case. Nothing here is advice, and nothing here suggests changing a
drug, a device or a therapy (ETHICS.md §3).

1. Is conditioned pain modulation, or any dynamic sensory test, measured in
   your service at all, and if so with which protocol and how repeatable is it
   in your hands?
2. Given that between-session reliability in patients is reported as fair at
   best, is there any decision for which you would rely on a single CPM
   result in one person?
3. In people with long-standing CRPS, does the published finding of intact
   inhibition at group level (in cohorts of 24 and 16) match what you see, and
   would you expect it to hold in someone whose main feature is touch-evoked
   allodynia, a profile that made up 22 of 604 in the largest clustering
   study?
4. When benefit from an implanted stimulator fades and hardware causes have
   been excluded, what do you consider to have changed: the stimulus reaching
   the target, the tissue around the lead, or the state of the nervous system
   being stimulated? Is there any measurement you use to tell these apart?
5. Is temporal summation or wind-up, which is the dynamic measure most
   consistently reported to change with stimulation, something that could be
   followed over time in people with a stimulator, as a research observation?
6. How do you separate the effect of sleep disruption on pain from the effect
   of pain on sleep in people whose pain is worse at night, and does it change
   what you would want measured?
7. Several commonly used drug classes are reported to move the CPM measure in
   one direction or the other in small studies. When interpreting any sensory
   test, how do you account for what the person is taking?
8. How much of the difference between open-label and blinded results for a
   therapy do you attribute to expectation, and does the evidence that
   expectation works through the same brainstem system change how you read an
   unblinded improvement?
9. If a research group proposed measuring dynamic sensory tests repeatedly in
   people with ganglion stimulators, what would make such a study acceptable
   and useful from where you sit?

## 9. Established / contested / speculative

**ESTABLISHED.** A supraspinal loop inhibits convergent dorsal horn neurons
when a noxious stimulus is applied elsewhere on the body; it is absent in the
spinalised rat (PMIDs 460935, 460936). The human counterpart is blocked by an
opioid antagonist (PMID 2168836). Placebo analgesia engages the same brainstem
stations (PMID 19709634). CPM is lower in chronic pain groups than in controls
on average, effect size .78 across 30 studies (PMID 22981090). Pooled
between-session reliability of CPM is "fair or less for all modalities"
(PMID 35142147). Some pain-free people show facilitation on a CPM test
(PMID 30699074). Expectation and attention move the CPM result
(PMIDs 26809850, 39541722). The DFNS QST battery, as its abstract lists it, does not contain CPM
(PMID 16697110). Chronic pain association signals are enriched in forebrain
neurons and in one nociceptor subtype of the human ganglion (PMID 41055971).
Explantation of implanted stimulators for diminished pain relief is common
over ten years in one large centre (PMID 39084704).

**CONTESTED.** Whether CPM is reliable enough for use in one person: one
review says it "is a reliable measure" with caveats (PMID 27559835), a later
meta-analysis and three responder studies say not (§2). Whether pre-operative
CPM predicts chronic post-surgical pain: positive in the founding thoracotomy
cohort and in one independent knee cohort, not found in two later chest-surgery
cohorts and in the largest knee cohort (§3.1). Whether CPM predicts drug
response, and in which direction (§3.2). Whether CPM is reduced in neuropathic
pain: reduced, unchanged and *increased* have all been reported (§4.4).
Whether sleep loss reduces CPM (§5.2). Whether baseline CPM predicts response
to spinal cord stimulation, and in which direction (§6.2). Whether spinal cord
stimulation changes CPM, given that every positive study is unblinded (§6.1).

**SPECULATIVE.** That a deficit in descending inhibition contributes to pain
in CRPS: two independent cohorts, of 24 and 16 patients, found none (§4.1). That descending modulation
explains fading benefit of ganglion stimulation: no data found (§6.4). That
baseline CPM predicts durability of ganglion-stimulation benefit: no data
found, and the claim as worded cannot be refuted (§8.3). That the link between
long-term opioid use and poorer ganglion-stimulation response runs through
descending modulation (§6.3). That inherited chronic pain risk acts in
brainstem modulatory cells: not found, and possibly not yet tested (§7.2).
That a sequence model can usefully prioritise pain-associated variants by
cell type: a company claim about a general capability, with no pain content
(§7.3).
