## 1. Diagnosis: a screening instrument doing the work of a diagnosis

**The Budapest criteria were built for sensitivity and behave accordingly.** Bruehl et al. (*Pain* 1999;81:147-54, PMID 10353502) showed the 1994 IASP criteria had sensitivity 0.98 but **specificity 0.36**. The Budapest revision (Harden et al., *Pain Med* 2007;8:326-31, PMID 17610454) was validated by Harden et al. (*Pain* 2010;150:268-74, PMID 20493633): clinical version sensitivity **0.99, specificity 0.68**; research version specificity 0.79.

The 5th-edition guidelines (Harden, McCabe, Goebel et al., *Pain Med* 2022;23(Suppl 1):S1-S53, PMID 35687369) call them "broad, inclusive and accessible **screening type** diagnostic criteria" and concede that "several larger trials have failed, probably due to using the broad general diagnostic criteria... in a multifactorial/multi-mechanism disease." That sentence is the field's own explanation for its treatment failures and it should be read before any of section 4.

**CRPS-I versus CRPS-II is admitted in the guideline text to be historical.** The distinction was retained "largely for historical reasons", the diagnostic signs are identical, and it rests on "nebulous definitions of what constitutes major nerve damage" tested by electrodiagnosis that is "almost always too painful" to perform. Gierthmühlen et al. (*Pain* 2012;153:765-74, PMID 22154921), the largest quantitative sensory testing dataset (298 CRPS-I, 46 CRPS-II), found "almost identical somatosensory profiles".

**Warm versus cold.** Bruehl et al. (*Pain* 2016;157:1674-81, PMID 27023422) confirmed a two-cluster solution in 152 patients, but median duration was 4.7 months for warm against 20 months for cold, so the split is heavily confounded with chronicity. The duration-matched study, Eberle et al. (*Neurology* 2009;72:505-12, PMID 19204260), is better evidence: cold CRPS carried more dystonia, more sensory loss and more prior chronic pain. Cold onset predicts poor outcome (PMID 19692800; PMID 15836983), but the 2021 consensus declined to formalise the subtypes.

**ICD-11 and the current revision debate.** The Valencia consensus (Goebel, Birklein, Brunner et al., *Pain* 2021;162:2346-8, PMID 33729210) deliberately **left the criteria wording untouched "so as to avoid invalidating the criteria"** and changed everything around them. CRPS was re-parented out of ICD-11's autonomic-disorder branch, called "a mistake based on the historic misunderstanding of CRPS as primarily an autonomic disorder", into **chronic primary pain, MG30.04**. CRPS-II was explicitly declared *not* a neuropathic pain condition. And a third subtype was created: **"CRPS with remission of some features"**, for patients who once met criteria but no longer do.

**That subtype is the correct label for most cases at ten years or more**, and applying it changes what the literature says about them. `[ICD-11 code confirmed via Barke, Korwisi, Rief, Clin Psychol Eur 2022;4:e9933, PMID 36760323; PARTIALLY UNVERIFIED against icd.who.int directly.]`

Live: Drummond, Gierthmühlen, Bultitude et al. (IASP CRPS SIG), "Are complex regional pain syndrome types I and II the same?", *Pain* 2026, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000004067, PMID 42475065 `[CONTENT UNVERIFIED, paywalled]`. Note also that the ASIPP 2025 diagnostic guidance (PMID 40773629) was **formally rebutted by the IASP SIG** (Bultitude et al., *Pain Physician* 2026;29:E257-9, PMID 42263319). Do not cite one without the other.

### Diagnostic instability is the strongest and least-discussed evidence in the field

- Mailis-Gagnon et al. (*Pain Med* 2014;15:1965-74, PMID 25339179), last author Harden: of 54 patients referred **as CRPS**, only **27.7% were confirmed**. Of the 39 non-CRPS patients, 74% had another diagnosable entity.
- Raasveld, Wolff, Luan, Valerio, Eberlin (*Clin J Pain* 2025;41:e1291, PMID 40211731): 53 CRPS-labelled patients at a peripheral nerve clinic. Budapest criteria had been assessed in only **26%** before referral. On blinded re-adjudication, 42% CRPS by all raters, **26% determined not to have CRPS**, 32% inconsistent. **In 33% of those labelled CRPS type I, an inciting nerve injury was identified.** Companion outcomes (PMID 40815056): 66% had peripheral nerve surgery and **67% of those benefited**.
- Kosy et al. (*J Knee Surg* 2018;31:797-803, PMID 29294499): prospectively, **zero** CRPS cases in 100 knee replacements by Budapest criteria, where 8 would have been diagnosed under the older Orlando criteria. Replicated at scale in **1,026 patients with zero cases** (PMID 42390455).
- del Piñal's series are the strongest surgical version. *Plast Reconstr Surg* 2023;152:807-16 (PMID 36952596): 225 consecutive referred-as-CRPS patients, all reallocated to named conditions. Prospective series (PMID 39570217): 82 patients, pain 8.9 to 0.6, **79% full resolution**. All Level II to IV, uncontrolled, self-referred.
- Independent corroboration that Budapest-positive patients can have a surgically correctable lesion: Lavigogne, Goubau et al. (Belgian), *Hand Surg Rehabil* 2026, PMID 42379301: 24 patients **all fulfilling Budapest criteria**, treated as irritative carpal tunnel syndrome, VAS 70 to 11 out of 100, 56% complete relief.
- Dellon, Andonian, Rosson (*J Foot Ankle Surg* 2010;49:33-6, PMID 20123284) is the lower-extremity version, 55% excellent and 30% good, but it is **circular by design**: patients were selected by nerve-block response and then reported to benefit from treating the block-responsive lesion, with 43% follow-up and an unvalidated outcome scale. No published methodological critique of the Dellon CRPS papers was found in PubMed, so this criticism is analytic rather than citable.

### Natural history, and why the two headline numbers contradict

Sandroni et al. (Olmsted County, *Pain* 2003;103:199-207, PMID 12749974): incidence 5.46 per 100,000 person-years, **74% resolution, often spontaneous**. de Mos et al. (Dutch GP database, *Pain* 2007;129:12-20, PMID 17084977): **26.2 per 100,000**, nearly five times higher, same era, same nominal criteria.

Schwartzman et al. (*Clin J Pain* 2009;25:273-80, PMID 19590474) reported **zero remission in 656 patients**, but the design enrolled only patients with more than one year of disease at a tertiary referral centre, which structurally excludes remitters. Bean et al. (*Eur J Pain* 2016;20:884-94, PMID 26524108): at one year, two-thirds still met Orlando criteria but only a quarter met Budapest **research** criteria and 5.4% were symptom-free.

## 2. Mechanism

**Peripheral small-fibre pathology.** Oaklander et al. (*Pain* 2006;120:235-43, PMID 16427737): epidermal neurite density reduced in 17 of 18 CRPS-I subjects, mean **29%**, with no reduction in symptom-matched chronic-leg-pain controls. But Kharkar et al. (PMID 22622910) found abnormality in only about **20% of 43 patients**, and Rasmussen et al. (PMID 30299507, n=8) found density reduced **bilaterally**, which contradicts the focal unilateral framing and, practically, **invalidates using the contralateral limb as a control**.

**Neurogenic inflammation.** Birklein et al. (*Neurology* 2001;57:2179-84, PMID 11756594) found CGRP elevated and normalising with treatment, but state that "pain and hyperalgesia, in particular in chronic stages, were independent of increased neuropeptide concentration". The definitive synthesis, Parkitny et al. (*Neurology* 2013;80:106-17, PMID 23267031), shows a **stage-dependent** profile in which **substance P is lower, not higher, in chronic blood**, cutting against a simple "neurogenic inflammation persists" account. Their own note: "most studies failed to meet 3 or more of our quality criteria".

**Autoantibodies: the most exciting and least-replicated line.** Kohr et al. (*Pain* 2011;152:2690-700, PMID 21816540) localised β2-adrenergic and M2 muscarinic epitopes to the second extracellular loop. Seropositivity is about 42 to 60% across studies (PMID 15534271; PMID 19375222; PMID 25250722), never near 100%. Passive transfer is real and mechanistically deep: Tékus et al. (PMID 24145209) and Helyes et al. (*PNAS* 2019, PMID 31182576), the latter showing IL-1-dependent, microglia-derived IL-1β mediation with anakinra reversing an established phenotype. Cuhadar et al. (PMID 31343542) showed nociceptor sensitisation with titre-severity correlation.

Four caveats must travel with this:

1. **Every passive-transfer paper originates from the interlinked Goebel, Helyes-Tékus or Clark-Kingery networks. No independent replication and no failure-to-replicate was found. The status is "unreplicated", not "refuted".**
2. All models require a **prior incision**. IgG alone was not shown sufficient and none claims spontaneous pain.
3. The field's own 2024 position statement (PMID 38873615) states: "The molecular binding targets of pertinent autoantibodies have not yet been identified."
4. **The definitive clinical test was negative** (the IVIG trial, section 4).

**Cortical reorganisation of S1: get this exactly right, because the strong form is not supported.** The originals (Juottonen 2002, PMID 12127033, n=6; Maihöfner 2003, PMID 14694034, n=12; Pleger 2005, PMID 15732114) inferred map *size* from the Euclidean distance between two digit dipoles.

Then Di Pietro et al. (*Hum Brain Mapp* 2015, PMID 25256887) compared **both** hands against controls and concluded: "**CRPS seems to be associated with an enlarged representation of the healthy hand, not a smaller representation of the affected hand.**"

And **Mancini, Wang, Schira, Isherwood, McAuley, Iannetti, Sereno, Moseley, Rae**, *J Neurosci* 2019;39:9185-96 (PMID 31570533) used 3T phase-encoded travelling-wave fMRI to measure cortical surface area, centroid location and map geometry **directly on the cortical surface** in 18 unilateral CRPS patients and 17 controls. Result: **no effect on area (F(1,28)=0.404, p=0.530), no centroid shift (p=0.890), Bayesian analysis favouring the null.** Verbatim: "The cortical map of the fingertips of the CRPS hand in S1 is strikingly comparable with the map of the unaffected hand and controls."

Precision matters in both directions. Mancini **excluded the thumb**, the very digit the classic MEG studies used; did not test within-map overlap or blurring; and had n=18 with weak Bayes factors. Their much-quoted "shrinkage with longer disease duration" **did not survive correction for multiple comparisons**. They also concede the treatments work: "Although these interventions appear to offer clinical benefit, they are unlikely to engender a 'restoration' of somatotopic representations in S1."

**The strong form of the claim is refuted. A weaker form is untested, not disproved.**

**Microglia.** Human TSPO PET in CRPS exists only from one Seoul National University group (PMID 28072713, n=11; PMID 29986072, n=12). **Alshelh has no CRPS TSPO study**, and attributing CRPS neuroinflammation imaging to that work is an error. Loggia (*Brain* 2015, PMID 25582579) is chronic **low back pain**, and its thalamic signal correlated *negatively* with pain, the opposite sign. The animal data constrain it further: Cropper et al. (PMID 31095093) found central microglial signal at 7 and 21 days **only**, with the marker restricted to endothelial cells at 7 weeks.

**Disuse.** Terkelsen et al. (*Anesthesiology* 2008, PMID 18648239): four weeks of cast immobilisation in 30 healthy humans produced cold and mechanical hyperalgesia and temperature asymmetry, **essentially all resolving by 28 days**. Guo and Kingery showed cast immobilisation **alone** reproduces warmth, oedema, allodynia and periarticular osteoporosis in rats (PMID 15109512). Against equating CRPS with disuse: van Velzen et al. (PMID 25451624) found corticospinal excitability **normal** in CRPS while genuinely immobilised fracture controls were abnormal.

## 3. Dystonia and fixed deformity

Movement disorders occur in **65%** of tertiary-referral CRPS patients, 91% of them dystonia (van Rijn et al., *Pain* 2007;130:287-93, PMID 17499924, n=185). Latency is highly variable: under a week in 26%, but **over a year in 27%**, which is the observation most resistant to a simple peripheral-injury account. About **25% of CRPS develops fixed dystonia** (Munts et al., *BMC Neurol* 2011;11:53, PMID 21609429).

**The dominant lower-limb pattern is plantar flexion of the toes.** Munts 2011 characterised 114 legs and 123 arms and found that only a model of **imbalanced inhibitory force feedback from Golgi tendon organs (the Ib pathway)** reproduced the observed postures. This was validated experimentally with robot manipulators by Mugge et al. (*IEEE TNSRE* 2016, PMID 25955788), which also found the deficit present in fixed dystonia **with or without CRPS**.

**Corrections to the usual account of intrathecal baclofen.** The NEJM paper is van Hilten et al. 2000;343:625-30 (PMID 10965009) with **n=7**, showing hand response but little leg improvement. The large study is **van Rijn** et al., *Pain* 2009;143:41-7 (PMID 19232828): single-blind dose escalation, 42 patients, 36 implanted, **positive but uncontrolled**, and the complication burden is the headline: **89 adverse events in 26 patients, 52 of them pump or catheter defects, 6 explants**. The **Munts** *Pain* 2009 randomised crossover (PMID 19683392) is **intrathecal glycine**, not baclofen, and was wholly negative. The negative baclofen RCT is van der Plas et al. (PMID 21332937).

**The functional overlap is genuinely unresolved.** Schrag, Trimble, Quinn, Bhatia (*Brain* 2004, PMID 15342362), 103 patients: 20% fulfilled CRPS criteria, **37% met criteria for documented or clinically established psychogenic dystonia**, dissociative disorders 42% against 0% in classical dystonia. But **10% of the prospective and 45% of the retrospective group had no evidence of psychogenic dystonia and no alternative explanation**. Verdugo and Ochoa (PMID 10639611) take the maximal sceptical position. The best modern framing is Popkirov, Hoeritzauer, Colvin, Carson, Stone, "CRPS and functional neurological disorders: time for reconciliation", *JNNP* 2019;90:608-14 (PMID 30355604), which rejects the malingering focus for a predictive-processing account. Prevalence is now firm: Steinruecke et al. (*JNNP* 2024, PMID 38383157) pooled 4,272 functional neurological disorder patients and found **CRPS diagnosed in 22% (95% CI 6-39)**, and, importantly, **most interventions did not improve pain even when other symptoms improved**.

### For a progressive curl deformity in a previously operated toe, two papers dominate and both are discouraging

> Gray, Welck, Cullen, Singh, "Functional dystonia in the foot and ankle", *Bone Joint J* 2021;103-B:1127-32 (PMID 34058886). 29 patients, mean 7.1-year diagnostic delay, 9 with coexisting CRPS. **Of 20 given botulinum toxin, 1 responded. Of 12 operated, NONE reported improvement.** At 3.2 years: 4 improved, 17 unchanged, 8 deteriorated. Verbatim: "**Surgery in this group of patients is not recommended and may worsen the condition.**"

Singh, *Foot Ankle Surg* 2022;28:691-6 (PMID 34649761), adds the decisive bedside sign: **the "fixed" deformity is usually correctable under general anaesthesia**. Botulinum non-response is not pharmacological: Schilder et al. (PMID 24532257) showed near-normal neuromuscular junction blockade in CRPS dystonic muscle.

There is **no RCT of botulinum toxin for CRPS dystonia**, no controlled trial of serial casting or manipulation under anaesthesia, and **no published series of flexor tendon lengthening in a CRPS limb**.

On re-operating a CRPS limb, the legitimate source is Veldman and Goris (*Unfallchirurg* 1995;98:45-8, PMID 7886464): **13% recurrence in 47 operated limbs** under a defined precaution protocol. **Do not cite Scott Reuben** (PMID 15590255 and PMID 16428550, both confirmed retracted for fabrication). The best modern number is Belgian: Hernigou et al., *JBJS Am* 2025;107:2352-8 (PMID 40924823): a history of CRPS raised post-operative CRPS from 8% to **32% (OR 5.4, 95% CI 2.57-11.4)**.

## 4. Treatment evidence

**The umbrella verdict.** Ferraro, Cashin, Wand, Smart, Moseley, McAuley, O'Connell, Cochrane overview, *CDSR* 2023;6:CD009416 (PMID 37306570): "**we identified no high-certainty evidence for the effectiveness of any therapy for CRPS**", and "current non-Cochrane systematic reviews of interventions for CRPS are of low methodological quality and should not be relied upon." **No RCT evidence exists for tricyclics or opioids at all.**

### Neuromodulation, and the Kemler arc

This is the single most important dataset in this brief.

| Timepoint | Result | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| 6 months | SCS+PT −2.4 cm against PT +0.2 cm (p<0.001), **no functional benefit**, 25% needed further procedures | *NEJM* 2000;343:618-24, PMID 10965008, open-label, 2:1, n=54 |
| 2 years | −2.1 against 0.0 | *Ann Neurol* 2004;55:13-18, PMID 14705107 |
| **5 years** | **"SCS+PT produced results similar to those following PT for pain relief and all other measured variables."** Favourable per-protocol subgroup p=0.06 | *J Neurosurg* 2008;108:292-8, PMID 18240925 |

And the line that says most about this field: "**Despite the diminishing effectiveness of SCS over time, 95% of patients with an implant would repeat the treatment for the same result.**" Cochrane reports **94% incidence of reoperation or reimplantation** for that 5-year study.

Independent long-term cohort: Geurts et al. (*Neuromodulation* 2013, PMID 23363081), 84 implanted CRPS-I patients to 12 years: **only 41% had at least 30% pain relief at endpoint**, with 122 reinterventions in 51 patients. Cochrane against sham (O'Connell et al., *CDSR* 2021;12:CD013756, PMID 34854473): **MD −8.73 out of 100, below the review's own clinical-importance threshold, very low certainty.**

### DRG stimulation, which is where this programme's interest sits

**ACCURATE** (Deer et al., *Pain* 2017;158:669-81, PMID 28030470): success **81.2% against 55.7% at 3 months, 74.2% against 53.0% at 12 months**. The caveats, stated plainly: **funded by Spinal Modulation, a wholly owned company of St. Jude Medical, with four device-company employees as co-authors**; "subjects, investigators, and study site staff were not blinded"; the primary endpoint is conditioned on successful trial and implant; and **DRG had markedly higher procedure- and device-related adverse event rates (46.1% against 26.3%, and 36.8% against 26.3%)**. Cochrane excluded it entirely as an active-comparator trial. **No 2-, 3- or 5-year ACCURATE follow-up exists, which is itself a finding.**

#### Correction, 2026-09-01: sham-controlled DRG evidence does exist

An earlier version of this brief stated that "no sham arm exists for DRG
stimulation anywhere". **That is too strong and is withdrawn.** It is true of
the *pivotal implantation* trial, and ACCURATE remains unblinded and
industry-authored. It is not true of the frequency literature, where two
randomised double-blind crossover trials in already-implanted patients include
a sham stimulation phase.

| Trial | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Chapman-style frequency trial, *Acta Neurochir* 2022, PMID 35217898, n=19 (7 with CRPS) | 20, 40, 60, 80 Hz and sham, randomised order, 4-day phases, 2-day washouts | **20 Hz best on every variable** (VAS, McGill, EQ-5D, Beck). VAS 3.7 at 20 Hz rising to **5.8 at 80 Hz**, significant across all measures, and the smallest increase in analgesic use |
| Low-frequency follow-up, *Acta Neurochir* 2023, PMID 36705762, n=17 | 4, 20, 60 Hz and sham, subthreshold amplitude, 5-day phases | 4 Hz VAS 3.8, 20 Hz 4.2, 60 Hz 4.6, **sham 5.3**. 4 Hz significant only against sham (p=0.001). Low frequency **not** significantly better than 20 Hz; the authors flag possible underpowering |

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

**Frequency specificity at the DRG has human blinded support, and this is the
sharpest contrast in the whole programme.** At the spinal cord, PROCO found 1,
4, 7 and 10 kHz equivalent once position and charge were controlled. At the
DRG, 20 Hz beats 80 Hz by more than two VAS points in a blinded crossover, and
every active frequency beats sham. Whatever is happening at the ganglion is not
the same as what is happening at the cord, and the T-junction filtering
mechanism (Chao et al., PMID 32658148) is a candidate explanation for why.

**But it is already implemented, so it is not an opportunity.** The 2023 trial
records that **"baseline stimulation frequency was 20 Hz in all patients"**.
Twenty hertz is the standard clinical DRG setting, not an untapped finding. Any
conjecture proposing 20 Hz DRG stimulation as a novel intervention is proposing
current practice.

The one genuinely open thread is the **4 Hz** trend: numerically better than
20 Hz on every measure, not statistically separable from it, in a trial the
authors themselves suspect was underpowered. That is a question worth asking,
not an answer.

**The habituation claim, and why C-001 should not lean on it.** Levy et al. (*J Pain* 2020;21:399-408, PMID 31494275) found SCS relief declining (66.9% at 1 month to 57.9% at 12) while DRG did not. But two Abbott employees are co-authors, the sponsor makes the DRG device, and it stops at 12 months.

**The independent long-term data look nothing like ACCURATE, and they point the other way.**

- Eldabe et al. (*Pain* 2022;163:702-10, PMID 35302973), 32 implanted, followed 5 to 7 years: **only 50% still using the device**; of the 16 survivors, **only 2 still had the original implanted pulse generator**; 8 explanted for dissatisfaction.
- Gatzinsky et al. (*Reg Anesth Pain Med* 2025;50:923-9, PMID 39084704), n=400: cumulative explantation **17% at 3 years, 23% at 5 years, 38% at 10 years**; explantation **specifically for diminished pain relief 10%, 14% and 23%**, with **DRG carrying a higher risk of that outcome than SCS**.
- Vanloon et al. (Belgian-led, *Neuromodulation* 2025;28:234-48, PMID 39601733), 634 participants: complications 37%, lead fracture 6%, migration 6%, **explantation 12%, primarily for insufficient relief**.
- Chapman et al. (*Neuromodulation* 2026, PMID 42439846), 375 leads: **lead fracture in 22.8% of patients**, Kaplan-Meier cumulative about 15% at 60 months.

**So the sponsor-authored 12-month claim that DRG does not habituate is contradicted by independent multi-year data in which DRG habituates more.** C-001 currently cites Levy for the habituation framing and must be revised accordingly: its premise survives and arguably strengthens, but one of its supports does not.

### Salvage, before explant. This is the most actionable finding in the brief.

> **Stimulation holiday.** D'Souza and Her, *Reg Anesth Pain Med* 2022 (PMID 35985769). **40 patients with loss of efficacy. Relief rose from 8.7% to 39.4% one month after a holiday, a mean difference of 30.6 points.**

> **Waveform conversion.** Systematic review of 22 studies and 1,591 patients: **77.9% of salvage trials reported successful** (Yener et al., *Neuromodulation* 2026, PMID 42249871).

DRG as salvage after failed SCS: Chapman et al. (PMID 35760751), 60 patients including 24 with CRPS, NRS 8.7 to 3.8 at 34 months, retrospective and Deer-coauthored. The formal algorithm is **NACC 2024** (Deer et al., *Neuromodulation* 2024;27:951-76, PMID 38904643), with the DRG-specific companion NACC 2019 (PMID 30246899).

**All salvage evidence is uncontrolled.** And note for Branch A: **ECAP closed-loop exists only for SCS, in back and leg pain, not CRPS. There is no closed-loop DRG system.** That absence is exactly the gap C-001 points at.

## 5. Why does chronic CRPS resist everything?

Five explanations, in descending order of evidential support.

1. **The diagnostic construct is too heterogeneous to be treatable.** This is the guideline authors' own explanation. A cohort assembled by an instrument with 0.68 specificity contains patients with focal nerve lesions, functional dystonia, resolving disuse and true CRPS, and no single agent works across that mixture. **Some fraction of "refractory CRPS" is refractory because it is not CRPS.**
2. **The mechanism that maintains chronic disease is not the mechanism that starts it.** Inflammation is stage-dependent with substance P *lower* in chronic blood; central microglial activation in the animal model is gone by 7 weeks; bone scintigraphy sensitivity decays with duration; trophic and vasomotor signs remit while motor and sensory signs persist (PMID 24530407). **Anti-inflammatory and anti-resorptive strategies are aimed at a window that closed years earlier**, which is exactly the pattern in the bisphosphonate data.
3. **The peripheral generator, if any, was never removed.** In 33% of type-I-labelled patients reaching a peripheral nerve clinic an inciting nerve injury exists, and neuroma surgery in the over-24-month and over-2-prior-operations subgroup still succeeds at meaningful rates (Poppler et al., PMID 29189515, pooled 77%). **This is PB-5, and it has better support than PB-5 was written with.**
4. **The cortical target that treatments aim at may not be abnormal.** If Mancini 2019 is right, sensorimotor retraining is not restoring anything, which fits the failed clinical-practice replication of graded motor imagery.
5. **Fixed dystonia has its own mechanism and its own poor prognosis**, in the Ib force-feedback loop, overlapping functional neurological disorder, where pain specifically does not respond to the interventions that help other symptoms.

## 6. The 2024-2026 frontier: thin, with three real items

- **Anti-CGRP: one trial, and it is real.** Thiyagarajah, Terkelsen, Birklein, Finnerup, Gylfadottir, *Brain Sci* 2025;15:468 (PMID 40426639): **protocol only**, n=60, fremanezumab against placebo, 8 weeks, CRPS duration 3 to 36 months, Aarhus and Mainz. **Registry ID `[UNVERIFIED]`. Do not cite a number for it.** `erenumab AND CRPS` returns **zero** PubMed records and zero registered trials.
- **IL-6 blockade: the premise appears to be mistaken.** Checked five ways: **there is no tocilizumab CRPS trial.** The likely source of the memory is **IL-1**: ISRCTN12908996 ("INCA"), anakinra in refractory CRPS, University of Liverpool, 26 enrolled, completed December 2024, **basic results posted to ISRCTN June 2025 and never published**. That is the single most under-reported item in this field.
- **JAK inhibitors: nothing clinical.** No CRPS trial, no case series. Rodent work only.
- **Low-dose naltrexone.** CRPS itself has 2 case reports (PMID 23546884). The analogue evidence has turned decisively negative: FINAL (PMID 38258677) and INNOVA (PMID 42385209) both null, with INNOVA's response tracking **belief about allocation**.
- **Plasma exchange, rituximab.** Aradillas et al. (PMID 26218942), retrospective, n=33. **Rituximab in CRPS does not exist.** Goebel's plasma-exchange trial was consulted about but never registered or run.
- **Vagus nerve stimulation.** The entire CRPS evidence base is one 20-patient study that is **not yet recruiting**.
- **Focused ultrasound.** Real momentum in chronic pain, **zero CRPS studies**. Anyone marketing it for CRPS in 2026 is extrapolating from healthy-volunteer work.
- **Closed-loop.** NCT06421350 (closed-loop SCS for CRPS, Scripps, recruiting); NCT04909138 (intermittent DRG stimulation, Rush); **NCT07170722, a sham-controlled sub-threshold DRG trial at Umeå, is COMPLETED and unpublished** and worth chasing.
- **Neridronate is the biggest industry story and the biggest evidence problem:** NCT07210515 "CRPS-RISE", phase 3, n=270, recruiting since April 2026, 24 US sites, launched into the teeth of the 2026 *Annals* meta-analysis showing about 10 points short-term and increased harms.
- **Reviews to anchor on:** Goebel A, "Complex Regional Pain Syndrome", *NEJM* 2025;393:2338-48 (PMID 41370799); Ferraro et al., *Lancet Neurol* 2024;23:522-33 (PMID 38631768). **There is no Nature Reviews Disease Primers CRPS entry and no discrete "2025 international CRPS guideline"** `[UNVERIFIED, likely does not exist]`. Both national guidelines are expired: AWMF 030-116 lapsed 7 January 2023; the RCP 2nd edition (2018) passed its 2023 review date unrevised.

## 7. Established / contested / speculative

**ESTABLISHED.** Budapest criteria have high sensitivity and mediocre specificity and are a screening tool. CRPS-I and II is a historical distinction with near-identical sensory profiles. Which criteria a patient meets depends on when you examine them. A large minority of CRPS-labelled patients have another diagnosis, often a treatable focal nerve lesion. **SCS in CRPS loses its advantage over physiotherapy entirely by 5 years.** Neurostimulation carries substantial hardware attrition. IVIG does not work in long-standing CRPS. Sympathetic blockade is not effective. Amputation carries about 47% CRPS recurrence and no demonstrated advantage. Surgery for functional or fixed foot dystonia does not help.

**CONTESTED.** Whether small-fibre loss is focal, unilateral or common. Whether S1 map reorganisation exists at all in the form claimed. Whether cold phenotype is prognostic or a marker of chronicity. Whether bisphosphonates have a clinically meaningful effect. Whether fixed dystonia in CRPS is organic, functional or a spectrum. **Whether DRG habituates less than SCS: the only supporting data are 12-month and sponsor-authored, and the independent multi-year data point the other way.** Whether graded motor imagery does anything outside its originator's trials.

**SPECULATIVE.** Autoantibody causation. Anti-CGRP, IL-1 blockade, low-dose naltrexone, vagus nerve stimulation, focused ultrasound, psilocybin and plasma exchange in CRPS. **Closed-loop DRG stimulation, which does not exist.** Topical phenytoin for CRPS, which has no CRPS evidence at all beyond two single-patient reports.

## 8. What a reference centre would re-run, and where those centres are

### 8a. The re-workup for long-duration post-surgical foot CRPS with a failing DRG stimulator

1. **Re-adjudicate the diagnosis formally**, by the Raasveld and Eberlin method (confirmed, inconclusive, or non-CRPS), and classify under the Valencia scheme rather than forcing a binary. At more than ten years the correct label is very often **"CRPS with remission of some features"**. Record which criteria are met *today*.
2. **Hunt the focal nerve generator. This is the highest-yield step and the one most often skipped.** Prior probability: 33% of type-I-labelled patients have an identifiable inciting nerve injury, and a quarter are not CRPS on blinded re-review.
   - **3T MR neurography** of the tibial nerve and its plantar branches, plus sural, saphenous and peroneal. Chhabra et al. (PMID 21701028) found **re-entrapment in 9 of 10 re-operated tarsal tunnels, overall accuracy 86%**, though only 67% for neuroma detection.
   - **High-resolution ultrasound**, which is complementary rather than redundant: Bianchi et al. (PMID 30215106) found nerve compression **missed by examination, radiographs and MRI** in 3 of 4 surgically proven cases.
   - **Controlled (comparative, not single) diagnostic blocks.** Uncontrolled blocks carry about a 30% false-positive rate (PMID 29913995). Carry the caveat from Yatziv and Devor (PMID 31095098): the DRG generates roughly three times more ectopic discharge than the neuroma site, so a **negative peripheral block does not exclude a peripheral lesion**.
   - **Do not rely on a normal nerve conduction study.** The AANEM review found only 4 Class III studies and could not determine sensitivity or specificity (PMID 16003732).
3. **Do not order a three-phase bone scan.** Sensitivity is duration-dependent and effectively spent after about 5 months (PMID 20173431: sensitivity 31-50%, duration the only significant predictor). **MRI cannot confirm CRPS** (PMID 41223627, sensitivity 6-91%) and is worth doing only to exclude alternatives.
4. **Characterise the deformity.** Test whether the toe curl is **correctable under general anaesthesia** (PMID 34649761). If it is, the fixed-dystonia pathway applies and the Gray and Singh data say **do not operate**.
5. **DFNS-protocol quantitative sensory testing** at a certified laboratory (PMID 16697110), reported against the new CRPS-specific three-cluster classification (Vollert, Baron, Forstenpointner, *Br J Anaesth* 2026, PMID 42538249, n=604). Be honest that phenotype-guided drug selection is a research strategy, not a standard: it worked for oxcarbazepine (PMID 25139589) and failed to replicate for lacosamide (PMID 37565715).
6. **Skin biopsy for intraepidermal nerve fibre density** to the EFNS/PNS guideline (PMID 20642627), with the caveat that reduction may be **bilateral**, so the contralateral limb is not a valid control.
7. **Interrogate the device before considering explant.** Impedances, lead position and integrity, then the **NACC 2024 salvage ladder**: reprogramming, waveform change, **stimulation holiday** (best value, zero cost, +30.6 points in D'Souza's series), lead revision, target conversion.
8. **If a focal generator is found and surgery is contemplated**, the applicable evidence for this subgroup is Poppler et al. (PMID 29189515). Realistic long-term expectation from the best lower-limb dataset (PMID 32513643, mean 8.9-year follow-up): **NRS 7.3 to 4.9; 59% improved, 19% with severe persistent pain.** Quote Hernigou 2025 on recurrence risk (8% to 32%, OR 5.4).
9. **Screen the amputation question out, not in**, and put Gilanyi's 47% recurrence in front of the patient.

### 8b. European reference centres

**Netherlands.** **Erasmus MC Rotterdam, Center for Pain Medicine** (Huygen, Dirckx, Mangnus, van der Spek) is by publication output the leading academic CRPS group in Europe. `[Formal "expertisecentrum" designation UNVERIFIED.]` **UMC Groningen** (Geertzen, Dijkstra) is the amputation-decision centre. **LUMC Leiden is a historical, not current, CRPS-dystonia centre**: van Hilten has published nothing on CRPS since about 2013.

**Germany.** **Universitätsmedizin Mainz** runs a named CRPS clinic under **Frank Birklein**, diagnostic and mechanistic rather than rehabilitative. **BG Universitätsklinikum Bergmannsheil, Bochum** runs an interdisciplinary CRPS clinic, inpatient multimodal therapy, and a **certified in-house QST laboratory that accepts external referrals**. Also Würzburg (Sommer, Rittner), Klinikum Fürth (Maihöfner, *not* Erlangen), Kiel (Baron, Gierthmühlen).

**United Kingdom, the only country with genuine formal commissioning.** **The Walton Centre, Liverpool**, with the University of Liverpool Pain Research Institute under **Andreas Goebel** (chair of the RCP guideline group and of the IASP CRPS SIG). **Bath National Pain Centre** is explicitly **NHS England-commissioned**, running multidisciplinary assessment then a **two-week residential intensive functional rehabilitation** programme, is **deliberately non-interventional**, and accepts private and international referrals.

**Belgium.** No CRPS designation exists and the rare-disease route is a dead end. But the **neuromodulation infrastructure is the strongest in Europe and is formally regulated**: **DRG stimulation is reimbursed specifically for CRPS of the lower limbs** under condition **B-§13**, requiring a trial of at least 21 days, psychological evaluation before and after, national advisory review, and registry entry, with applications through the **Neuro-Pain platform**. **Any DRG revision must be done at one of the 30 RIZIV-authorised hospitals** (list valid from 1 December 2025). Relevant centres: **VITAZ Sint-Niklaas** (Smet, with Van Buyten as consultant), **AZ Delta Roeselare** (Billet), **ZOL Genk** (Van Zundert), **UZ Brussel STIMULUS** (Moens, Goudman), **UZ Antwerpen** (Hans), **UZ Leuven** (Morlion, Theys), and **Cliniques universitaires Saint-Luc / UCLouvain** (Berquin, Legrain, Mouraux), which is the de facto Belgian CRPS academic group. Note that the federal pain-policy note of 27 November 2025 records that only **16 of 35** multidisciplinary pain centres met minimum criteria in 2023.

**Elsewhere.** **Balgrist, Zurich** (Brunner) runs an evaluated interdisciplinary inpatient programme and produced the two best diagnostic-accuracy papers cited here. **CRPS falls under no European Reference Network**, and **there is no EU CRPS registry**.
