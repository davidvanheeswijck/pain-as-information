# E-12. Lumbar stenosis and nerve-root compression in CRPS-labelled lower-limb pain

> Evidence brief, 23 November 2026. An evidence addition under D-004
> (`ledger/DECISIONS.md`; intent:
> `intent/evidence/2026-11-23-lumbar-root-compression-brief.md`). It registers
> no prediction, files no conjecture and runs no simulation.
>
> **What this is.** A review, at population level, of one question the earlier
> briefs did not ask: whether a problem at the lumbar spine (narrowing of the
> central canal, of the lateral recess or of the foramen, or a disc herniation
> pressing on a nerve root) is found in people labelled with CRPS of the foot
> or leg, as a mimic or as a contributor; and what a narrowed foramen means
> for a stimulation lead that sits in it. It follows the conventions of E-11:
> one row per observation, the best number found, where that number was read,
> and why it may not transfer.
>
> **Not medical advice.** Nothing here is a diagnosis, a recommendation or a
> device instruction (ETHICS.md §1 and §3). No dose and no stimulation setting
> is given. Where a quoted passage or table contained one, the quotation stops
> before it. Imaging with an implanted stimulator is described at class level
> only: what guidance documents say in general, never what applies to a
> particular device. The closing sections phrase what follows as questions a
> treating team might consider.
>
> **How it was built.** Every identifier was resolved live on 2026-11-23
> against NCBI E-utilities or the ClinicalTrials.gov v2 API. Nothing is cited
> from memory. **23 full texts were retrieved through `efetch db=pmc` and
> read in part or in whole; 64 further sources were read as abstracts
> only.** "In part" matters: the long guidance documents were searched by
> term and the passages found were read, not the whole text (appendix B).
> Thirteen publisher pages were requested by script for sources with no PMC
> copy: five were refused (HTTP 403), seven returned a redirect page with no
> article text, and one returned an abstract page without the article text.
> The brief was revised after review on the same date; row R10, query Q39
> and the reading of all Q6 titles date from that revision.
> Five cited sources have a PMC record that returned no body text through
> `efetch`, and eight have a PMC copy that was not read (appendix C). "Not
> read in full" therefore means not retrieved or not read by these routes,

>

## Bottom line

1. **How often a lumbar root problem sits behind a CRPS label of the lower
   limb is not known.** No cohort was found by these queries in which people
   labelled with CRPS of the leg or foot had the spine examined or imaged
   and the yield for stenosis or root compression reported. One small series
   of 12 such people who had a lumbar MRI was found (row R10, abstract); it
   measured the size of the ganglion and its abstract reports nothing on
   stenosis. The re-adjudication studies E-05
   starts from were read as abstracts only. One of them names radiculopathy as
   an example of the alternative diagnoses and gives no count for it. The
   others do not mention the spine. "The abstract does not mention the spine"
   is not "the spine was not examined".
2. **The diagnostic criteria were validated against a group that included
   radiculopathy, and a third of that group met them.** In the validation
   study of the Budapest criteria (full text), the comparison group was 47
   people with other neuropathic pain, 30% of them with radiculopathy, and the
   clinical criteria had a specificity of 0.68. The label alone therefore does
   not exclude a root problem. The same full text shows that weakness and
   altered reflexes, which are the classical root signs, were common in the
   CRPS group too.
3. **The reverse direction is real and poorly counted.** CRPS-type pain is
   reported after lumbar spine surgery, and occasionally with a disc
   herniation alone, in case reports and small series. No incidence with a
   denominator was found. In one claims database of 650,562 people operated
   for lumbosacral radiculopathy, about 1.1% also carried a CRPS code (full
   text; the percentage is this brief's sum of two table cells).
4. **No bedside or laboratory test separates a root source from a CRPS-type
   source with known accuracy in this population.** Accuracy figures exist for
   the straight-leg raise, the neurological examination, electrodiagnosis and
   the selective nerve-root block. Every one was measured in people suspected
   of a spinal cause, mostly surgical candidates, and none in people labelled
   with CRPS. Specificity of the root block is low and uncertain. The
   straight-leg raise is positive in about a third of people with a narrowed
   foramen.
5. **Imaging stenosis is common in people without symptoms.** In a
   community sample scanned by CT, 22.5% of people without low back pain had
   a narrowed canal by the looser of two cut-offs and 6.0% by the stricter
   (full text). In a Japanese population sample, 30.4% had severe central
   narrowing on MRI and 17.5% of those had symptoms (abstract). A narrowed
   canal or foramen on a scan is therefore weak evidence that it is the
   source of pain.
6. **For ganglion stimulation, a severely narrowed foramen is named as a
   contraindication in one safety document and as a level to avoid in one
   consensus document.** For both, the full text was retrieved and the
   passages were found by term search; neither was read end to end. No study
   was found that
   tests whether foraminal narrowing predicts failed lead placement, lead
   migration, lead fracture or loss of benefit. The one analysis of fracture
   predictors that was found (abstract) did not list foraminal anatomy among
   the variables it tested.
7. **Imaging the spine with an implanted stimulator is constrained.** Guidance
   (full text retrieved; passages found by term search) says most systems
   are conditional for MRI with restrictions,
   and one consensus document names CT with a myelogram as the alternative
   when MRI cannot be done. One abstract reports that the requested MRI was
   within the device approval in 2 of 26 examinations in people with a
   ganglion stimulator. One abstract reports two cases of paraplegia after CT
   myelography in people with a thoracic stimulator and thoracic stenosis.
8. **Whether the caution about operating on a limb with CRPS carries over to
   spinal surgery has not been studied**, as far as these queries show. The
   figures E-05 records are from limb surgery.
9. **For the three questions of D-003:** a root or foraminal source was not
   mapped by E-11 under Q-A. It would behave like E-11's ganglion candidate
   under a peripheral nerve block and differs from it in being a structural
   lesion that can be imaged. For Q-B, a new or progressing root problem
   recorded as fading benefit is a possibility no source found here
   examines, and it shares an obstacle with E-11's lead-position
   explanation: both need imaging that the device restricts. This brief adds
   evidence; it does not extend the programme's set of hypotheses (D-004),
   and nothing found here ranks anything.

## How to read the tables

**Columns.** As in E-11. (1) The observation. (2) What it would separate, and
in which direction. (3) Best source or sources. (4) Source status, exactly one
tag. (5) The number, verbatim with its denominator, the kind of number it is,
the population, and whether that population matches. (6) Independence: group
lineage, sponsor, replication. (7) The main reason the number may not
transfer.

**Status tags.** Defined in E-11, "How to read the tables", and used
unchanged. `number read in full text`: the passage was read in the open full
text and is quoted. `number from abstract only`: a number exists and was read
only in the PubMed abstract. `direction only`: the source gives a direction
and no usable number, or a number for only one side of the comparison the row
is about; a statement in a guidance document is tagged this way. `no data
found`: nothing on point came back from the logged queries, including a
broader cross-check. A `no data found` row is a statement about those
queries, not about the literature (EPISTEMICS.md rule 11).

**Population match.** The population the question has in mind is
long-standing, post-surgical, lower-limb, CRPS-labelled pain, and for the
device rows a ganglion-stimulation implant. Each row says which of those
features the source population has. "Not stated" means the passage read did
not say.

**Terms.** *Central canal stenosis*: narrowing of the canal that holds the
nerve roots. *Lateral recess stenosis*: narrowing of the side channel a root
crosses before it leaves. *Foraminal stenosis*: narrowing of the opening
through which the root leaves; in the lower lumbar spine this is usually
where the dorsal root ganglion lies (row F3). *Radicular pain*: pain from a
nerve root. *Neurogenic claudication*: leg symptoms brought on by standing or
walking and eased by sitting or bending forward, the classical picture of
central stenosis.

**Row letters.** R, a root problem behind the label. S, CRPS after spine
surgery or radiculopathy. T, tests. F, the foramen and the ganglion. M,
imaging with an implant. L, treatment of lumbar stenosis. They do not collide
with E-11's A, B and C.

**What section 1 says, in plain terms.**

- The number the question asks for was not found. No study found here took
  people labelled with CRPS of the leg or foot, looked at the lumbar spine in
  all of them, and reported how many had a narrowed canal or foramen or a
  compressed root. One series of 12 people with a lumbar MRI was found; its
  abstract reports a smaller ganglion on the affected side and nothing on
  stenosis (R10).
- Single cases exist in which a CRPS-type picture of the foot went with a
  disc fragment in or beside the foramen that the first imaging had missed
  (R9). They show the possibility, not its frequency.
- Whether the spine was examined or imaged in the three re-adjudication
  sources E-05 starts from: not stated in any of their abstracts. All three
  were read as abstracts only. One names radiculopathy among the alternative
  diagnoses, without a count (R1). The other two are from limb nerve and hand
  surgery settings and do not mention the spine (R2, R3).
- Two full texts re-examined CRPS-labelled people with tests aimed at nerves
  (R5, R6). Both found many nerve diagnoses. Neither reports a lumbosacral
  root diagnosis, and neither reports imaging the spine. **Reading:** these
  studies looked where their method points, at limb nerves; they do not show
  that root problems are rare.
- The diagnostic criteria do not protect against a root problem by
  themselves. Radiculopathy patients were part of the group the criteria were
  tested against, and the criteria's specificity against that group was 0.68
  (R4). The protection is meant to come from the exclusion rule, whose
  performance is not measured anywhere found here.
- The guidance documents list radiculopathy as something to exclude. They do
  not say how, or how often it is found.

## 2. The reverse direction: CRPS after lumbar spine surgery, or with radiculopathy as the inciting event

The question: is CRPS reported after lumbar spine surgery, or with
radiculopathy as the event that set it off, and how good is the evidence?

**Evidence grade.** Case reports and small uncontrolled series, plus one
single-centre retrospective cohort of 35 people. No prospective cohort and no
incidence with a denominator was found. On the usual scales this is the
lowest grade: it shows that the sequence is reported, not how often it
happens.

  resolved without a second operation; the time of resolution or improvement

  narrowed foramen the picture is different: pain at rest is the commonest
  finding, which CRPS shares (T1, T2).

  two-thirds of confirmed lumbar root pain was non-dermatomal in the one
  study found (T3).

  and positive in only a third of people with foraminal stenosis (T2, T4).

  found. A narrowed canal or foramen on a scan is common enough that finding
  one does not show it is the source of pain.

   lumbar MRI in lower-limb CRPS reports ganglion size only (R10).
3. *It is mechanistically close to G, and distinct from it.* In the rat, a
   narrowed foramen makes the ganglion fire (F1), so a foraminal lesion can
   be described as "G with a mechanical cause". It differs from G as E-11
   uses the term in three ways: it is a structural lesion that imaging can
   show; it can be posture-dependent where the canal is involved (T1); and it
   has its own treatment literature (section 6).
4. *The observations E-11 mapped do not separate it from G.* Under a complete
   peripheral nerve block (E-11 rows A1, A2, A16), pain generated at the root
   or ganglion would be expected to persist, exactly as for G. Under a block
   at the root (E-11 row A7; T8 here), pain from the root, from the ganglion
   and from the limb would all be expected to go. So neither block
   distinguishes a root source from E-11's three. This is C-009
   applied one level up.
5. *The observations that might separate it have no measured accuracy in
   this population.* Posture dependence, dermatomal pattern, straight-leg
   raise, reflexes and electrodiagnosis were all measured in people suspected
   of spinal disease (T1 to T8, T11), and the classical root signs are common
   in CRPS-labelled people (T5).
6. *Imaging cannot settle it alone.* Narrowing is common without symptoms
   (T9, T10), so a positive scan is weak evidence for a root source and a
   normal scan is the more informative result. No sensitivity of imaging for
   a painful root lesion in this population was found.

Mixed states are not excluded by any of this. Section 2 shows the labels
coexisting: about 1.1% of people operated for radiculopathy carried a CRPS
code (R7), and CRPS is reported starting after spine surgery (S1 to S3). A
root lesion and a CRPS-type state in the same limb is a possibility the
published record neither counts nor rules out.

### Q-B: lead position and foraminal anatomy

E-11 lists three explanations for fading benefit: **H**, habituation; **L**,
lead or hardware drift; **D**, disease progression. It adds that expectation
wearing off is a fourth it could not separate.

**Reading: the rows touch Q-B at four points, none of them tested.**

1. *A new or progressing root problem could be recorded as fading benefit.*
   If narrowing develops or worsens over the years of an implant, new pain in
   the same limb would look, from the outside, like the device working less
   well. In one series of a different device, 5.7% of implanted people later
   had spine surgery, mostly for stenosis (F9). No study was found that looks
   for this in people with a ganglion stimulator (F10). Under E-11's scheme
   this would be filed under D, although it is a different process from
   progression of CRPS, with different implications.
2. *The lead and the ganglion share a fixed space.* Guidance from two
   societies and one adverse-event analysis reason that a lead in a narrow foramen can
   itself press on the nerve (F4, F5). That is a hazard described at
   placement. Whether a foramen that narrows after placement changes how a
   lead performs was not found.
3. *Foraminal anatomy has not been tested as a predictor of lead migration,
   fracture or outcome* (F6, F7). The one analysis of position within the
   foramen found it related to the power needed and not to clinical outcome,
   and it left out the people in whom the therapy had failed (F8).
4. *A root source and E-11's lead explanation share an obstacle.* Both are
   questions about structure near the foramen, and both need imaging that the
   implant restricts (section 5). The guidance sentence on worsening pain
   names imaging for lead location only (F10). **Reading:** when benefit
   fades, the imaging that would show a displaced lead and the imaging that
   would show a new narrowing are, in part, the same examinations, and E-11
   found no figure for how often either is done.

It does not bear on H in any way found here.

### Q-C

Nothing found here bears on whether allodynia and background pain separate.
One animal result touches it: compression of the ganglion produced withdrawal
to light stroking as well as other pain behaviour (F1), which says a
foraminal lesion can produce touch-evoked responses in a rat, and nothing
about people.

### What E-11's bottom line looks like with this added

E-11 concluded that the data to rank its observations do not exist. This
brief adds evidence on a source E-11 did not map and does not change that

sections 1 to 5 has a likelihood that could be attached to it in the

## What reading the full text changed

Places where the full text differed from, or added to, what an abstract
said. Listed because a reader who has only the abstracts would be misled.

1. **The comparison group behind the criteria's specificity.** The abstract
   of the validation study (PMID 20493633) says "non-CRPS neuropathic pain".
   The methods give the mix: radiculopathy 30%, and a lower-limb location in
   74.4% of that group against 47.7% of the CRPS group (R4). E-05 §1 quotes
   the 0.68 without the mix.
2. **Root signs in the CRPS group.** The same full text's Table 2 shows
   altered reflexes in 50.8% and sensory loss to light touch in 57.7% of the
   CRPS group (T5). This is not in the abstract.
3. **Foraminal stenosis review: three versions of one figure.** Rest pain is
   74% from 4 studies in the abstract, 73% in the results text and 72 in
   Table 10, and Table 4 lists five studies. The extension test's n = 537 in
   the abstract does not match the 511 that its table rows sum to (T2).
4. **Neurological examination review: figures credited to the wrong
   reference standard.** The abstract (PMID 28231784) gives the headline
   sensory accuracy "using MR imaging as a reference standard"; Table 3
   attributes it to the one study with a surgical reference. The abstract's
   "mean" straight-leg raise sensitivity and specificity are, in Table 6, the
   sensitivity of one study and the specificity of another (T5).
5. **Dermatomal study: the denominator.** The abstract's 64.1% "of cases" is
   ambiguous between patients and nerve roots. Table 2 shows it is 66 of 103
   patients (T3).
6. **Community CT study: the age figures differ.** The abstract of PMID
   19398386 gives acquired stenosis by the looser cut-off as 20.0% under 40
   and 47.2% at 60 to 69. The results text gives a rise "from 16.0% to 38.8%"
   between under 40 and 60 and over (T9). The age bands differ; the youngest
   band does not, and its two figures disagree.
7. **Review of CRPS after spine surgery: 20 articles or 10.** The abstract
   says 20 articles were retrieved. The results analyse 10 papers and 72
   patients, and the table behind them is an image (S2).
8. **Root block review: how thin the specificity is.** The abstract gives
   pooled specificities. The full text adds that four cohort studies
   "contributed a total of just 10 true negative cases" (T8).
9. **Stimulation guidance: no contraindication statement where one might be
   expected.** E-05 and E-11 cite the ASPN best-practice guideline for
   ganglion stimulation. A term search of its full text found foraminal
   stenosis treated as an obstacle and a hazard, not listed as a
   contraindication. The contraindication is stated in the safety practices
   of a different society, IPSIS (F4). Because this rests on a term search,
   a statement phrased in other words could have been missed.

## Established / Contested / Speculative

**ESTABLISHED** (replicated, or read in a full text with no contrary source
found).

- Narrowing of the lumbar canal on imaging is common in people without
  symptoms and rises with age. Three unrelated population studies agree in
  direction, with different definitions (T9).
- The straight-leg raise, used alone, does not settle whether a lumbar root
  is compressed. Two systematic reviews 16 years apart agree: sensitive and
  unspecific in surgical series in the first, "low diagnostic accuracy" in
  the second (T4). Both were read as abstracts.
- A selective nerve-root block has low and uncertain specificity. Two
  independent systematic reviews agree (T8).
- The CRPS criteria do not, by their sign and symptom rules, exclude people
  with radiculopathy: specificity 0.68 against a comparison group that was
  30% radiculopathy (R4). One study, read in full, by the criteria's authors.
- CRPS-type pain is reported after lumbar spine surgery, in case reports and
  series from several countries over four decades (S1 to S3).
- At the fourth and fifth lumbar levels the ganglion usually lies in the
  foramen (F3).
- MRI in people with an implanted stimulator is restricted by conditions, in
  the passages found in all three guidance documents searched, which come
  from two societies (M1).

**CONTESTED** (sources disagree, or one source with a clear limit).

- Whether surgery does better than non-operative care for lumbar stenosis.
  The largest trial favours surgery on pain and not on function by assigned
  group; the second large trial finds no difference; the Cochrane review has
  "very little confidence" either way (L1, L2).
- How firmly severe foraminal stenosis rules out ganglion stimulation. Two
  bodies have written on it. IPSIS lists it as an absolute contraindication.
  ASPN, in a manufacturer-funded consensus, says to avoid the affected level
  and consider an adjacent one, and in its best-practice guideline does not
  list it as a contraindication in the passages found. The three ASPN
  documents share authors, so this is one society's position stated with
  different emphasis in different places, set against one other society's.
  It is a difference of degree between two bodies, not a count of documents,
  and neither cites a study (F4).
- How well reflex change, weakness and sensory loss detect a lumbar root
  lesion. The accuracy figures in T5 are from one systematic review of 12
  studies (Tawa 2017, full text), whose abstract calls reflex testing
  "notably good" on specificity while its tables show sensitivities from
  0.14 to 0.67. The Cochrane review's abstract gives no figures for these
  signs and one sentence: "diagnostic performance of most physical tests
  (scoliosis, paresis or muscle weakness, muscle wasting, impaired reflexes,
  sensory deficits) was poor". The two characterisations differ, and the
  Cochrane full text was not read (T4, T5).
- How often root pain follows a dermatome. One study says a third of the
  time; it was not replicated here (T3).
- Whether needle electromyography is specific enough to help. A review says
  "modest sensitivity but high specificity"; the one matched series, for limb
  nerves, found a sensitivity of 45% against a nerve block in 22 people; the
  CRPS guideline calls electrodiagnosis "almost always too painful" (T6).

**SPECULATIVE** (a mechanism or an inference, without a test in people).

- That compression of the ganglion in a narrowed foramen maintains
  long-standing pain in people. Shown in rats by one group; inferred in two
  small human studies (F1, F2).
- That a root or foraminal lesion sits behind some share of long-standing
  CRPS-labelled lower-limb pain. No count exists (R8).
- That the ganglion is smaller on the affected side in lower-limb CRPS, and
  that this reflects atrophy. One abstract, 12 people, internally unclear
  figures, no replication (R10).
- That a new or progressing foraminal narrowing explains some fading of
  benefit from ganglion stimulation. Not examined in any source found (F10).
- That lead problems reduce access to MRI in people with ganglion
  stimulators. An inference from two abstracts about another device (M3).
- That the ratio of foramen to ganglion size predicts anything. A published
  proposal without outcome data (section 4).
- That the caution about operating on a limb with CRPS applies, or does not
  apply, to spinal surgery. Not studied (L4).

## Questions a treating team might consider

These are questions, at population level, that the rows above raise. They are
not steps, and none is a recommendation (ETHICS.md §3). Each names the row or
source it comes from. Risk, burden, cost and availability of any observation
are outside this brief, and so is every individual's circumstance. Nothing
here concerns what to do with any device.

**On whether the root was ever part of the picture (section 1)**

1. When a CRPS label of the leg or foot was given or confirmed, was a
   lumbar root problem considered under the rule that no other diagnosis
   explains the picture better, and on what observations? The published
   record does not say how often it is, and the criteria's own specificity
   against a group that included radiculopathy was 0.68. (R4, R8.)
2. Were the re-assessments that looked for another cause aimed at the limb
   nerves only? The studies of that kind found here examined the limb and do
   not report the spine. (R2, R5, R6.)
3. If there was an operation on the lumbar spine before the limb pain began,
   how was a remaining or recurrent root compression told apart from CRPS
   starting after surgery? Both are reported, and no abstract found says how
   the distinction was made. (S1, S2, S3, S6.)

**On what the bedside observations can and cannot carry (section 3)**

4. Does the pain change with posture, in the way described for central
   stenosis, and how much does the answer mean when pain at rest is the
   commonest finding in foraminal stenosis? (T1, T2.)
5. How much weight can a non-dermatomal pain pattern carry against a root
   source, when about two-thirds of confirmed lumbar root pain was
   non-dermatomal in the one study found? (T3.)
6. Can a reflex change, weakness or sensory loss be read as a root sign in
   someone who meets the CRPS criteria, given how often the same findings
   were recorded in the CRPS group of the validation study? (T5.)
7. What would a positive selective nerve-root block establish beyond
   dependence on input through that root, given that it also silences the
   ganglion and everything from the limb? And what is a single such block
   worth, with pooled specificities of 22%, 50% and 57% in three analyses of
   people suspected of a root problem? (T8; E-11 row A7.)
8. If a scan shows a narrowed canal or foramen, what is known about how
   often such a finding is the source of pain, when 22.5% of people without
   back pain had a narrowed canal by one definition and 21.9% of a population
   sample had substantial foraminal narrowing? (T9, T10.)

**On the foramen and the lead (section 4)**

9. In people considered for a ganglion stimulator, how is the foramen at
   the intended level assessed on imaging, and what counts as severe
   narrowing? Guidance from two societies treats severe narrowing there as a
   contraindication or as a level to avoid; neither defines severe. (F4.)
10. Do the trial results for ganglion stimulation describe people with a
    narrowed foramen at all, when the pivotal trial excluded people whose
    imaging was judged to contraindicate a lead? (F5.)
11. In people with a ganglion stimulator who report less benefit over time,
    how often is a new or progressing narrowing at the root looked for,
    alongside lead position, habituation and progression of the original
    condition, and how often is one found? No study was found that reports
    either. (F9, F10; E-11 rows B8, B14.)

**On imaging (section 5)**

12. In people with a ganglion stimulator, which imaging of the lumbar spine
    is generally available, and how is that determined from device
    labelling? In one series the requested MRI was within the device approval
    in 2 of 26 examinations. (M1, M2.)
13. If MRI is not available, what is known about the alternatives named in
    the guidance, CT with a myelogram and removal of the device, including
    the two reported cases of paraplegia after myelography in people with a
    thoracic stimulator and thoracic stenosis? (M4, M5.)

**On treatment, if a root problem were found (section 6)**

14. What do the trials of surgery for lumbar stenosis say for someone who
    also has CRPS-labelled pain in that limb, when none of them selected such
    people? (L1, L2.)
15. Is anything known about whether spinal surgery worsens CRPS in the limb?
    These queries found no study; the figures that exist are for surgery on
    the limb itself. (L3, L4.)

## What this brief cannot say

- **It cannot say anything about a person.** Every number is a group
  proportion, mean or accuracy figure. None is a likelihood for an
  individual. Whether a root problem is present in any one person is a
  clinical question this brief does not touch.
- **It cannot say how common the thing it is about is.** The central number,
  the share of CRPS-labelled lower-limb pain with a root or foraminal
  contributor, was not found (R8). The brief shows the question is open, not
  that the answer is "often" or "rarely".
- **It is not exhaustive, and it has already been shown to miss things.**
  Thirty-nine logged queries, screened by title, with abstracts read for the
  plausible hits. The review of this brief found a relevant cohort (R10)
  that the first 38 queries did not surface, because they required stenosis
  or root terms that its abstract does not use. Where a query returned more
  records than were listed, only the first 25 to 60 titles by relevance were
  read; appendix A gives the count for each query. For the broadest
  cross-check (Q6) all 263 titles were read after review; for the others the
  remainder was not read. Each `no data found` row is a statement about the queries named
  in it. A result reported inside a review, a surgical series, a supplement
  or a full text that was not retrieved would not have been seen.
- **The sources E-05 starts from were not read in full.** The three
  re-adjudication sources (R1, R2, R3) have no PMC copy and their publisher
  pages refused the scripted request or were not requested. "Not stated in
  the abstract" is all that can be said about whether the spine was examined
  in them. A person with library access could settle this, and it is the
  cheapest way to improve this brief.
- **The committee consensus documents were not read.** The two documents E-05
  cites by the committee name, on ganglion stimulation and on salvage, are
  not in PMC. What they say about foraminal stenosis, imaging or worsening
  pain is unknown here. The guidance whose full text was retrieved, and
  searched by term, comes from two other bodies: three of the four documents
  are from ASPN, with overlapping authors, one of them supported by a grant
  from the manufacturer; the fourth is from IPSIS. So the guidance evidence
  in this brief is two societies' positions, not four independent ones.
- **Full text was read in part.** For most sources the methods, results and
  tables were read; for the long guidance documents, only passages found by
  term search. A term search finds the words searched for. A statement about
  foraminal narrowing phrased in other words would have been missed.
- **Device labelling was not read.** Section 5 reports what articles say
  about a class of device. No manufacturer manual, regulatory summary or
  labelling was retrieved, and labelling changes over time. Nothing in
  section 5 says what applies to any device.
- **Accuracy figures do not transfer.** Every test accuracy in section 3 was
  measured in people suspected of a spinal cause. Applied to people labelled
  with CRPS, the same tests could perform better or worse, and nothing found
  says which.
- **"Stenosis" is several things under one word.** Central, lateral recess
  and foraminal narrowing have different symptoms and different evidence, and
  the prevalence studies use different definitions. For lateral recess
  stenosis one review states that no accuracy study was found (T2).
- **Disc herniation was covered thinly.** The question named it; the searches
  were weighted towards stenosis. The straight-leg raise and block rows are
  mostly about herniation. Treatment trials for herniation were not reviewed.
- **Animal work gives direction only.** One rat lineage (F1).
- **No guideline recommendation on surgery was read** (section 6).
- **One trial was not consulted by instruction.** A sham-controlled trial of
  ganglion stimulation cited in E-05 and E-11 was not read, fetched or
  searched for. It is not a source for any row here.
- **It ranks nothing and registers nothing.** No candidate is placed above
  another. No prediction is registered or scored, no conjecture is filed and
  no simulation is run (D-004).
- **Searches are dated 2026-11-23.** Several sources are 2026 papers; their
  final versions may differ.

**A miss, found in review, and why it happened.** The review of this brief
pointed to a cohort that the first 38 queries had not surfaced: 12 people
with CRPS of one lower limb and a lumbar MRI (now row R10). Q39 is the broad
query that returns it, run after review. The earlier checks missed it for
two reasons. First, every query aimed at R8 required a stenosis, root or
disc term, or a misdiagnosis term, and the abstract of that paper uses none
of them: it is about the size of the ganglion. The question had been framed
around the lesion and not around the examination. Second, the one query
written without field tags (Q6) also required a root or stenosis term, and
only its first 40 of 263 titles had been read. After review all 263 Q6
titles were read. The paper is not among them, which confirms the first
reason. Reading them did surface five further records, all from before 1999,
whose abstracts were then read: three single cases now in R9 and two reports
now in S5. For the other queries that returned more records than were
listed (Q2, Q4, Q5, Q12, Q20, Q24, Q27, Q29, Q34) the remaining titles were
not read.

**Broad cross-checks aimed at the negation.** For each `no data found` row
the table names narrow queries and at least one deliberately broad one: R8
(Q1, Q3 narrow; Q2, Q4, Q5, Q34, Q36, Q32, Q6 and Q39 broader), S4 (Q26 narrow;
Q2, Q27 and Q6 broader), T11 (Q31 narrow; Q1, Q2 and Q6 broader), F6 and F7
