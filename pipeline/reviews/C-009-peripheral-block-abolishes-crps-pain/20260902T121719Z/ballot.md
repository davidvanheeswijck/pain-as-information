## Claim

In patients with CRPS of more than five years' duration, a single
ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block proximal to the affected region
abolishes both spontaneous pain and mechanical allodynia within thirty minutes,
at plasma anaesthetic concentrations too low to produce a systemic effect. The
abolition occurs regardless of how much central sensitisation the patient
shows on quantitative sensory testing. Established CRPS is therefore maintained
by ongoing primary afferent input, not autonomously generated in the central
nervous system.

## Why this, why now

**This conjecture exists because the identical experiment has already been run
in a neighbouring population and gave an unambiguous answer, and nobody has run
it here.**

Haroutounian, Nikolajsen, Bendtsen, Finnerup, Kristensen, Hasselstrøm and
Jensen, *Pain* 2014;155:1272-9 (PMID 24704366), studied 14 patients with
peripheral neuropathic pain, 7 of them with **unilateral foot pain from
peripheral nerve injury**. Each received an ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve
block and an intravenous lidocaine infusion in randomised order, with full
quantitative sensory testing.

> "The peripheral nerve block resulted in a **complete abolition of ipsilateral
> pain within 10 min (median) in all patients**, with lidocaine plasma
> concentrations being too low to account for a systemic effect of the drug."

And their conclusion, which is the sentence this conjecture is built on:

> "regardless of the individual somatosensory phenotype and **signs of central
> sensitization, primary afferent input is critical for maintaining neuropathic
> pain** in peripheral nerve injury and distal polyneuropathy."

**CRPS was not in that study.** The claim that it generalises is currently
carried by a narrative review and a single case report:

- Baron, Hans & Dickenson, *Ann Neurol* 2013;74:630-6 (PMID 24018757), state
  that "In postherpetic neuralgia and complex regional pain syndrome, for
  example, these symptoms are maintained and modulated by peripheral
  nociceptive input." That is an assertion by authoritative people, not a
  measurement.
- Kato et al., *Pain Med* 2013;14:293-6 (PMID 23198747), report one CRPS
  patient in whom an ultrasound-guided rectus sheath block produced
  "disappearance of the spontaneous pain and allodynia". n=1, incidental, and
  unblinded.

**Why the gap matters more here than anywhere else in this programme.** The
ledger's standing on HC-2 says peripheral readability is the weakest hard-core
commitment, because in allodynia the traffic that hurts arrives on Aβ fibres
and the discrimination is made centrally (E-01 §5, §6). Haroutounian's result
does not contradict that account of *mechanism*, but it does contradict its
practical implication: even with central sensitisation demonstrably present,
removing the peripheral input removed the pain. If that holds in CRPS, then
every peripheral intervention this programme has considered is aimed at a real
target. If it fails in CRPS, then long-duration CRPS is the case where the
central changes have become autonomous, and the peripheral programme should
stop.

**Why not already done.** The obvious reason is that CRPS is diagnostically
unstable, so a block that abolishes pain is read as evidence the diagnosis was
wrong rather than as evidence about CRPS. That circularity is exactly what this
design has to break, and it is why the diagnosis must be independently
re-adjudicated before the block rather than revised after it.

## Mechanism

Ongoing discharge in primary afferents, whether from a neuroma, a sensitised
nociceptor population or ectopic activity at the dorsal root ganglion, supplies
the input that maintains a sensitised dorsal horn. The central changes are
real, measurable and necessary for allodynia, but they are a *gain* applied to
an input rather than a generator in their own right. Remove the input and the
gain has nothing to act on.

Timescales are ordinary and follow from pharmacology rather than from anything
exotic: local anaesthetic block of a peripheral nerve trunk reaches full effect
in 10 to 30 minutes, lasts 2 to 8 hours for the agents in question, and is
fully reversible. The prediction is a step change in pain on that timescale,
not a gradual decline.

## Forbidden observation

In a patient with CRPS of more than five years' duration and a technically
confirmed peripheral nerve block, spontaneous pain will not persist at more
than half its pre-block intensity while sensory block is demonstrably complete
in the painful territory.

## Killer

Prospective, within-patient, and cheap because the intervention is a standard
procedure already performed for other reasons.

**Population.** 20 patients with CRPS of the foot or hand, duration greater
than five years, meeting Budapest **research** criteria on independent
re-adjudication by two assessors blinded to the study hypothesis. That
re-adjudication happens **before** the block and is not revised afterwards,
which is what breaks the circularity described above.

**Intervention.** Ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block proximal to the
affected region, with a documented plasma anaesthetic concentration drawn at
the time of maximal effect. On a separate day, in randomised order, an
intravenous infusion delivering a comparable plasma concentration without
regional block. **The intravenous arm is the control that matters**: it
reproduces the systemic drug exposure without removing the afferent input, so
a pain reduction present in both arms is a systemic drug effect and not
evidence for the conjecture.

**Outcomes**, pre-registered, primary first: spontaneous pain on an 11-point
scale at 30 minutes; mechanical allodynia threshold; quantitative sensory
testing before and during block; and documented completeness of sensory block,
because an incomplete block is the obvious route to a false negative and must
be excluded before a non-response is interpreted.

**Refutation threshold.** The conjecture is refuted if the median reduction in
spontaneous pain under complete peripheral block is **less than 50%**, with the
95% confidence interval excluding an 80% reduction, in patients whose sensory
block is documented as complete. It is also refuted, differently and more
interestingly, if the intravenous arm matches the block arm, because that would
mean the effect was systemic all along.

Approximate cost **60,000 to 120,000 euro and 12 months**, in a centre that
already performs ultrasound-guided regional blocks and quantitative sensory
testing. This is the cheapest decisive experiment this programme has yet
proposed, and it is cheap because it is a diagnostic procedure being used as a
measurement rather than a new technique being built.

## Rivals

- **Long-duration CRPS is the case where central changes have become
  autonomous.** Haroutounian's patients had identifiable nerve injury or
  polyneuropathy and a median duration far shorter than five years. Duration,
  not diagnosis, may be what determines whether peripheral input still
  matters. *Distinguished by:* stratifying by duration within the sample, and
  by the fact that this rival predicts a graded failure with years since onset
  rather than a uniform null.
- **The pain that is abolished is not the pain that defines CRPS.** A block
  abolishes ongoing nociceptive input, but the disabling features of CRPS
  include motor, autonomic and trophic signs that a 4-hour block cannot
  address, so the conjecture could be true and clinically nearly useless.
  *Distinguished by:* measuring the non-pain Budapest features during block,
  which this design should do rather than treating pain as the whole disease.
- **Diagnostic reallocation, not mechanism.** Patients whose pain is abolished
  by a peripheral block had a focal nerve lesion all along and were mislabelled,
  which the reallocation literature says is true of 26 to 33% of long-standing
  CRPS labels (E-05 §1). *Distinguished by:* the blinded pre-block
  re-adjudication, and by reporting the block response separately for patients
  in whom a discrete lesion is and is not identifiable. **This rival is the
  most likely to be partly true, and the design should be read as measuring how
  much of CRPS it accounts for rather than as refuting it.**

## Severity

Given the conjecture is false, the probability the proposed test still comes out
favourable is about **0.2**.

The dominant route to a false pass is the systemic effect of the local
anaesthetic, and it is closed directly by the intravenous arm at matched plasma
concentration, which is the control Haroutounian's design already included and
which is the reason that study is quotable at all. The second route is
expectancy: a patient whose limb goes numb knows it, and a block cannot be
blinded. That is not fully controllable, and it should be stated as a permanent
limitation of this design rather than papered over. It is partly bounded by
using the intravenous arm, which also produces perceptible systemic sensations,
as the comparator rather than an inert placebo.

The third route is selection: recruiting from a surgical clinic would enrich for
exactly the patients with findable peripheral lesions. Recruitment must
therefore be from a general pain clinic population, consecutively.

## What it would change

**If confirmed**, the peripheral programme is vindicated for the population it
most cares about, and the immediate consequence is clinical rather than
theoretical: a peripheral nerve block becomes a **diagnostic test** that tells
an individual patient whether their pain has a peripheral driver, and therefore
whether peripheral intervention is worth pursuing at all. That is a cheap,
available, same-day answer to a question patients currently wait years for. It
would also restore HC-2 from "weakest commitment, four independent hits" to
something with direct human support.

**If refuted**, the result is more valuable still, and this is the more likely
outcome for the strongest version of the claim. It would mean established CRPS
is the case where central changes have become genuinely autonomous, that the
programme's peripheral branch is aimed at the wrong level for this population,
and that the reasonable targets are central. It would also mean the field
should stop offering peripheral procedures to long-duration CRPS patients on a
mechanistic rationale that does not hold, which is a claim with immediate
consequences for people currently being offered them.

Either way the answer arrives for well under a tenth of the cost of the animal
studies this programme was proposing two days ago, from a procedure that is
already performed daily.
