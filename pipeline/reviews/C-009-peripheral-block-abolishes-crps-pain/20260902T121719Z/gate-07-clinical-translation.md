# Translational Medicine Review — C-009

## 1. The patient population

**Phenotype.** Adults with unilateral foot or hand pain meeting Budapest *research* criteria for CRPS, duration >5 years, with spontaneous pain and mechanical allodynia, re-adjudicated by two assessors blinded to the study hypothesis *before* the block and not revised afterwards.

**Enrichment strategy.** The conjecture's own design is the enrichment: the block itself is the selector. That is both the strength and the weakness. The strength is that it is a within-patient, same-day, reversible test of mechanism. The weakness is that the population is defined by a diagnostic label that the conjecture itself acknowledges is wrong 26–33% of the time (the diagnostic reallocation rival). The design handles this by pre-block re-adjudication and by reporting block response separately for patients with and without an identifiable discrete lesion. That is the right structure, but it means the *effective* sample size for the mechanistic claim is not 20 — it is 20 minus the reallocated fraction, split across two strata. The study is powered for the headline, not for the stratified analysis that actually answers the question.

**Measurable criterion.** Budapest research criteria, independently applied. This is as good as the field has. It is not a biomarker, and the conjecture does not pretend otherwise.

**Assessment.** The population is specified as well as the diagnostic state of the art permits. The enrichment strategy is honest about its own circularity and has a pre-committed structure to break it. The residual problem is statistical power within strata, not population definition.

## 2. Endpoint and the placebo problem

**Primary endpoint.** Spontaneous pain on an 11-point NRS at 30 minutes post-block. Secondary: mechanical allodynia threshold, QST during block, documented completeness of sensory block.

**Blinding.** This is the central problem, and the conjecture states it plainly: **a peripheral nerve block cannot be blinded**. The patient's limb goes numb. There is no sham that produces regional anaesthesia without regional anaesthesia. The intravenous arm is a comparator, not a sham — it produces systemic sensations but not a numb limb, and the patient will know the difference.

**What this means.** The evidential standard achievable here is **open-label with an active comparator**, not randomised sham-controlled. That is a lower standard, and the conjecture says so. The intravenous arm controls for the *pharmacological* confound (systemic lidocaine effect), which is the most important one and the one that would produce a false positive for the mechanism. It does not control for expectation, and in a population with 5+ years of pain, expectation effects on a dramatic same-day intervention are substantial.

**Regression to the mean.** The design recruits patients with ongoing pain, not patients in flare, so regression to the mean is less severe than in flare-recruited trials. But any patient presenting for a study of this kind is presenting at a time when their pain is bad enough to motivate participation, so some regression is inevitable. The within-patient design with a separate-day intravenous arm partially bounds this, because regression to the mean would be expected to affect both arms.

**The honest statement.** This design can establish that regional block abolishes pain at plasma concentrations that do not, when delivered systemically. That is a real and important finding. It cannot establish that the abolition is not partly expectation-mediated. The conjecture acknowledges this. The question is whether the mechanistic claim — "primary afferent input is necessary for maintaining the pain" — requires the expectation-free version of the result. It does not, because the intravenous arm is the discriminator that matters: if systemic lidocaine at matched concentration does nothing and regional block abolishes pain, the difference is the removal of afferent input, regardless of what expectation contributes to the absolute magnitude of the block effect.

## 3. Risk

**Direct harm from the procedure.** Ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block is a routine procedure with a well-characterised risk profile: vascular puncture, intraneural injection, local anaesthetic systemic toxicity, nerve injury (transient or, rarely, persistent), infection. The rates are low in experienced hands — persistent neurological deficit is on the order of 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 30,000 for ultrasound-guided blocks. For a 20-patient study, the expected number of serious procedure-related adverse events is small but not zero.

**Disease-specific risks.** This is the part that matters and the part the conjecture does not address. CRPS is a condition in which **any intervention on the affected limb can trigger a flare or spread**. The literature on operating on CRPS-affected limbs is a warning, and a nerve block is not surgery, but it is an intervention on the affected limb. The needle itself, the injection, the transient paraesthesia — all of these are potential triggers. The conjecture should state this explicitly and include a pre-specified flare-monitoring and management plan. The risk is probably low for a single block, but it is not zero, and in a population with 5+ years of CRPS, a flare is a meaningful harm.

**Loss of function.** The block is temporary (2–8 hours). There is no permanent loss of function from the block itself unless there is nerve injury, which is rare. The temporary loss of protective sensation during the block is a fall risk and should be managed.

**Making the disease worse.** This is the live risk. A single diagnostic block is lower risk than a therapeutic series or a surgical intervention, but the conjecture should not assume it is zero. The design should include a pre-specified criterion for stopping the study if a flare signal emerges.

**Opportunity cost.** For a patient with 5+ years of CRPS, the established options are limited and the window of treatable disease is not short — this is not a population where a year spent on the wrong thing costs a cure. The opportunity cost of a single diagnostic block is low. The opportunity cost of the *conclusion* is higher: if the study is read as vindicating peripheral intervention in CRPS, patients may be offered peripheral procedures on the strength of a 20-patient open-label study. That is a downstream risk the conjecture should acknowledge.

## 4. Regulatory path

**Route.** This is a clinical investigation using an approved drug (local anaesthetic) for an approved procedure (peripheral nerve block) in an approved indication (regional anaesthesia). It is not a drug trial, not a device trial, and not a combination product. It is a **mechanistic clinical study** using standard-of-care procedures.

**Regulatory category.** In the EU, this would be a clinical investigation of a medicinal product used within its approved indication, which falls under the Clinical Trials Regulation (EU) 536/2014 if it is interventional, or it may be classifiable as a non-interventional study if the block is being performed for diagnostic purposes as part of standard care. In the US, it would be an investigator-initiated study under an IND if the intravenous arm is considered experimental, or it may be exempt if both arms are standard care. The regulatory burden is **low** — this is not a novel therapy seeking approval.

**First-in-human timeline.** Not applicable in the usual sense. The procedure is already performed daily. The study could start within 6–12 months of protocol finalisation and ethics approval.

**Cost.** The conjecture's estimate of €60,000–120,000 is realistic for a single-centre study of this size, dominated by personnel time (two blinded assessors, QST technician, anaesthetist, study coordinator) and the cost of the intravenous lidocaine arm (which requires monitoring). The regulatory cost is minimal.

**Assessment.** This is the cheapest and fastest regulatory path of any conjecture in this programme, because it is a measurement using existing tools, not a new intervention.

## 5. Comparator

**The honest comparator.** The conjecture's comparator is the intravenous lidocaine arm at matched plasma concentration. That is the right comparator for the *mechanistic* claim. But the translational question is different: what is the best available current care for this phenotype, and what improvement over it would be clinically meaningful?

**Best available current care for CRPS >5 years.** The evidence base is thin. Current care includes: physical therapy (graded motor imagery, mirror therapy), psychological intervention (CBT, acceptance-based), pharmacological management (gabapentinoids, SNRIs, topical agents, occasional opioids), and in selected cases spinal cord stimulation or DRG stimulation. The effect sizes are modest. SCS in CRPS achieves roughly 50% pain reduction in 50–60% of implanted patients at 12 months, with a 30–40% explant/revision rate over 5 years. DRG stimulation is better for foot CRPS but still leaves a substantial minority with no meaningful benefit.

**What this study adds.** This study is not a treatment. It is a diagnostic test. The clinically meaningful outcome is not "does the block reduce pain" — it is "does the block response predict response to subsequent peripheral intervention, and does it do so better than current practice." That question is not answered by this study. The study establishes the *premise* for a diagnostic test; it does not validate the test's predictive value.

**Minimum clinically important difference.** For a diagnostic test, the MCID is not a pain score — it is a predictive accuracy. The conjecture does not state what predictive accuracy would be clinically useful. That is a gap, but it is a gap for the *next* study, not this one.

**Effect size.** The conjecture's refutation threshold is a median reduction of <50% with the 95% CI excluding 80%. That is a reasonable threshold for the mechanistic claim. It is not a threshold for clinical utility, and the conjecture does not claim it is.

## 6. Deliverability

**Affordability.** The procedure is cheap. Ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block is available in every hospital with an anaesthesia department. The intravenous lidocaine arm requires cardiac monitoring but is also routine. The total cost per patient is on the order of €1,000–2,000, dominated by personnel time.

**Availability.** The skills required — ultrasound-guided regional anaesthesia, QST, Budapest criteria assessment — are available in any tertiary pain centre. This is not a three-centres-worldwide intervention.

**Acceptability.** For patients with 5+ years of CRPS, a single diagnostic block is acceptable if the rationale is explained honestly: "This will tell us whether your pain is being maintained by input from the limb, which would mean peripheral treatments are worth pursuing, or whether it has become independent of the limb, which would mean they are not." That is a question patients want answered. The risk of flare is real and must be disclosed.

**What it would take to make it a treatment.** The block itself is not a treatment — it lasts hours. The treatment would be the peripheral intervention that follows a positive block: pulsed radiofrequency, peripheral nerve stimulation, or in selected cases surgical intervention on an identifiable lesion. The diagnostic test is only useful if it predicts response to those treatments. That validation study is the next step and is substantially more expensive and longer.

---

VERDICT: MINOR — The design is sound for its mechanistic claim, but the conjecture should state the flare risk explicitly with a pre-specified monitoring plan, and should acknowledge that the stratified analysis (lesion-identifiable vs. not) is underpowered at n=20, so the diagnostic-reallocation rival will not be fully resolved by this study alone.
