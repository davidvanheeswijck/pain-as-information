- **C-001** was killed because:

  > “The T-junction filtering mechanism is real but the conjecture treats C-fibre traffic as a labelled line for pain, which the evidence does not support; in established neuropathic pain the relevant traffic is on Aβ fibres and the relevant pathology is central, so gating C-fibre propagation at the T-junction does not address the mechanism of the disease.”

## Established field and established terminology

This belongs to **clinical pain medicine, regional anesthesia, and mechanism-based diagnosis of neuropathic pain**.

The established terms are:

- **Peripherally maintained pain** or **peripheral input-dependent pain**
- **Dynamically maintained mechanical allodynia**
- **Diagnostic somatic/afferent nerve block**
- In a related but importantly different literature, **sympathetically maintained pain**, tested using sympathetic blockade

“Central sensitization is present but requires continuing peripheral input” is not a new conceptual category. It is the definition of dynamically or peripherally maintained pain.

## Prior work

### Historical and canonical work

Local and regional blocks have been used to interrogate causalgia and reflex sympathetic dystrophy since the early clinical literature summarized by Livingston and Bonica. There is no defensible priority claim for the general maneuver in 2014.

The canonical modern mechanistic paper is:

- **Gracely RH, Lynch SA, Bennett GJ. “Painful neuropathy: altered central processing maintained dynamically by peripheral input.” Pain. 1992;51:175–194.**  
  It described pain and mechanical allodynia extending beyond the injured nerve territory, yet disappearing when the relevant peripheral input was blocked. The important conclusion was precisely that extensive central processing can remain *dynamically maintained* by peripheral traffic.

Haroutounian et al. 2014 supplied a better-controlled modern version, including systemic lidocaine exposure and quantitative sensory testing. It did not originate the concept.

### Work in CRPS itself

The statement “nobody has run it here” is too broad.

Somatic regional blockade has repeatedly been used in CRPS/RSD, including:

- Brachial plexus and sciatic/peripheral catheter blocks used to reduce pain sufficiently to permit rehabilitation
- Continuous peripheral nerve blocks in pediatric CRPS
- Case reports and small series of focal blocks, including the cited Kato et al. case
- Extensive sympathetic-block literature in CRPS, although that blocks a different target and cannot answer the afferent-necessity question cleanly

What appears not to be established is the **specific protocol** proposed here: independently adjudicated, long-duration Budapest-criteria CRPS; randomized crossover against matched systemic local-anesthetic exposure; documented complete somatic blockade; and prespecified spontaneous-pain response.

That is an incremental methodological delta, not a new biological conjecture.

## Failed and inconclusive attempts

### Sympathetic blocks

The closest large literature is negative or inconclusive.

- **Price et al., Clin J Pain 1998;14:216–226** found that both local-anesthetic and saline sympathetic injections could produce substantial short-term responses; local anesthetic mainly prolonged analgesia. This is direct evidence that an invasive, perceptible block procedure has a large nonspecific component.
- **O’Connell et al., Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016;CD004598** found the evidence for local-anesthetic sympathetic blockade in CRPS to be sparse and low or very low quality, without reliable evidence of superiority to placebo or other treatments.
- Intravenous regional sympathetic blockade with agents such as guanethidine was widely tried and largely abandoned after controlled trials and systematic reviews failed to show convincing benefit.

These failures do not refute somatic afferent blockade, but they show why an intravenous comparator alone is insufficient. It controls systemic local-anesthetic exposure; it does not control expectation, numbness, procedural salience, motor interruption, sympathetic efferent blockade, or changes in blood flow.

### Regional somatic blocks

Published therapeutic case series show that regional blocks can facilitate rehabilitation in selected CRPS patients, but responses are variable and populations are highly selected. This is already inconsistent with the title’s unqualified implication that a block simply “abolishes pain in long-duration CRPS.”

Examples include continuous peripheral nerve block reports in pediatric CRPS, particularly **Dadure et al., Anesthesiology 2005;102:387–391**. These are useful feasibility evidence, not proof of universal peripheral maintenance.

### Neuromodulation as an adjacent long experiment

The neuromodulation industry has effectively tested whether interrupting or modifying segmental input provides durable benefit in selected CRPS:

- **Kemler et al., N Engl J Med 2000;343:618–624** found short-term benefit from spinal cord stimulation plus physical therapy.
- At five years, the randomized-group advantage was no longer clinically compelling, although selected implanted patients often remained satisfied: **Kemler et al., J Neurosurg 2008;108:292–298**.
- The **ACCURATE trial** found dorsal-root-ganglion stimulation superior to conventional SCS for selected CRPS/causalgia patients: **Deer et al., Pain 2017;158:669–681**.

The recurring lesson is selection and durability, not universal response. A short successful block is not automatically a durable treatment predictor.

## Adjacent fields with the same structure

- **Epilepsy surgery:** a transient anesthetic or stimulation response can identify a necessary node, but does not by itself establish the origin of the pathological state.
- **Cardiac electrophysiology:** entrainment or temporary block can demonstrate circuit dependence without proving that the blocked site initiated the arrhythmia.
- **Cochlear implants and peripheral nerve stimulation:** removing or replacing peripheral input tests channel dependence, while decoding content is a separate problem.
- **Responsive neurostimulation and DBS:** acute suppression during stimulation often predicts neither chronic efficacy nor avoidance of adaptation.
- **Diagnostic medial branch and nerve-root blocks:** false-positive rates are sufficiently important that comparative or repeated blocks are commonly used. A single unblinded block is not treated as a specific diagnostic assay.

C-009 should import the distinction between **necessary conduit**, **generator**, and **treatment target** from these fields.

## Patents and industry

A diagnostic local-anesthetic block is mature, generic clinical practice rather than a product category with a plausible new device patent.

Relevant commercial programmes instead concern intervention after patient selection:

- **Spinal Modulation/Abbott Axium DRG stimulation:** developed for focal neuropathic pain and CRPS, supported by ACCURATE, acquired by St. Jude Medical and commercialized after FDA approval.
- **Abbott, Boston Scientific, Medtronic, and Nevro:** spinal cord stimulation platforms used in chronic neuropathic pain, including selected CRPS patients.
- **SPR Therapeutics SPRINT, Bioness/StimRouter, Nalu, and Curonix:** percutaneous or implanted peripheral nerve stimulation systems, with CRPS appearing mainly in case-level or mixed neuropathic-pain experience rather than a definitive CRPS pivotal trial.

Patent families in this area cover ultrasound-guided needles, peripheral nerve stimulation leads, DRG targeting, closed-loop stimulation, and stimulation waveforms. I find no meaningful product or patent novelty in “use a proximal local-anesthetic block to test whether pain is peripherally maintained.” The potentially publishable novelty is the controlled clinical protocol.

The most informative industry trajectory is SCS: encouraging early controlled results, substantial implantation, then attenuation of the randomized advantage at long follow-up. DRG stimulation improved anatomical targeting and short-term comparative outcomes, but does not establish that a one-time block predicts durable response.

## Problems with the present claim and experiment

1. **Mechanical allodynia is not an independent endpoint during complete sensory block.**  
   If the patient cannot receive the mechanical test stimulus, evoked mechanical pain necessarily disappears. Spontaneous pain is the informative endpoint.

2. **A positive result establishes dependence, not origin.**  
   It would show that the present pain state requires traffic through the blocked nerve. It would not distinguish ongoing ectopic discharge, ordinary background afference amplified centrally, sympathetic effects, motor input, or altered circulation.

3. **It does not test HC-2.**  
   HC-2 says pain-relevant structure is readable outside the CNS. A total conduction block tests whether the channel is necessary, not whether pain can be decoded from it.

4. **QST cannot establish “how much central sensitization” a patient has.**  
   QST can provide a sensory phenotype and evidence compatible with central amplification. It cannot quantify whether a central generator is autonomous.

5. **The claim, forbidden observation, and statistical threshold disagree.**  
   “Abolishes” suggests near-complete individual response. The proposed refutation rule permits many nonresponders so long as the median reduction remains sufficiently large. The conjecture must choose between:
   - a universal or high-responder-fraction claim, or
   - a population-average treatment-effect claim.

6. **The block must cover the whole painful territory.**  
   A single distal named-nerve block will not cover many CRPS distributions. A brachial-plexus block, or sciatic plus saphenous block, also introduces motor and autonomic effects that complicate interpretation.

7. **Post-randomization exclusion for incomplete block can bias the estimate.**  
   Technical success should be a prespecified estimand or principal-stratum analysis, with all randomized attempts reported.

8. **Twenty patients cannot support the proposed duration analysis.**  
   The claim is specifically about duration beyond five years, yet the design proposes an underpowered within-sample duration gradient. Duration should either be abandoned as a moderator or addressed through prespecified duration strata in a larger study.

9. **Diagnostic use requires validation, not merely mechanism.**  
   To call the block a diagnostic test for selecting peripheral intervention, the study must show that block response predicts a later, independently assessed outcome after a peripheral treatment. Otherwise it is only a pharmacological challenge test.

## Novelty verdict

**INCREMENTAL.**

The biological content—central amplification dynamically maintained by peripheral input, tested using afferent blockade—is established and has been named for more than three decades.

The honest novelty clause is:

> **A randomized matched-systemic-exposure somatic-block study restricted to independently adjudicated CRPS lasting more than five years.**

That is worth testing if the primary claim is narrowed to the reduction of **spontaneous pain**, the inference is limited to moment-to-moment peripheral dependence, and “diagnostic test” is withheld until predictive validity is demonstrated.
