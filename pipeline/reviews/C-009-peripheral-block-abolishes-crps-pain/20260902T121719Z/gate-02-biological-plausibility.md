# Biological Plausibility Review — C-009

## Where the conjecture is right

The conjecture correctly identifies a genuine and under-exploited feature of the physiology: **the dependence of central sensitisation on ongoing peripheral drive**. The dorsal horn is not a bistable memory element. Wind-up, central sensitisation, and the receptive field expansion that characterise established neuropathic pain are all *state-dependent* phenomena that require ongoing afferent input to be expressed. This is why Haroutounian et al. (2014) found what they found, and why the result is not surprising to anyone who has recorded from dorsal horn neurons during and after a peripheral block: the sensitised state collapses when the input is removed, because the sensitisation is a *gain change*, not a *generator*. The conjecture's framing of central changes as "a gain applied to an input rather than a generator in their own right" is, for the dorsal horn at least, substantially correct. The proposal to test this in long-duration CRPS with a properly controlled design is a legitimate and cheap experiment that would produce useful information either way.

---

## 1. THE WIRE

The conjecture does not propose to *read* anything from a nerve. It proposes to *block* a nerve and observe the behavioural consequence. This is the one design in the programme so far that sidesteps the wire problem entirely, and it should be credited for that.

However, the wire problem still constrains the *interpretation*, and the conjecture does not reckon with it.

A peripheral nerve block at the trunk level is **not selective**. It blocks everything: Aβ, Aδ, C, and the autonomic fibres travelling with the somatic trunk. The conjecture's mechanism section speaks of "ongoing discharge in primary afferents, whether from a neuroma, a sensitised nociceptor population or ectopic activity at the dorsal root ganglion" — but the intervention cannot distinguish among these, nor between nociceptive and non-nociceptive afferents. This matters because of what the ledger already knows (C-001, C-003): in established allodynia, the traffic that hurts arrives on **Aβ fibres**. A trunk block abolishes Aβ traffic too. So the experiment, even if it produces a positive result, cannot tell you *which* afferent class was maintaining the pain. It can only tell you that *some* peripheral input was necessary.

This is not fatal to the conjecture as stated — the claim is about *whether* peripheral input maintains CRPS, not *which* input — but it is fatal to any downstream inference that the result vindicates a nociceptor-targeted intervention. The conjecture's "What it would change" section says a positive result "would restore HC-2 from 'weakest commitment, four independent hits' to something with direct human support." It would not. HC-2 is about *readable structure* distinguishing nociceptive from innocuous traffic. A trunk block that silences everything cannot bear on that question. The conjecture should not claim that it can.

**Specific physiology:** A mixed peripheral nerve at the trunk level contains Aβ fibres (6–12 µm, myelinated, 35–75 m/s), Aδ fibres (1–5 µm, thinly myelinated, 5–35 m/s), C fibres (0.2–1.5 µm, unmyelinated, 0.5–2 m/s), and postganglionic sympathetic C fibres. Local anaesthetic block affects all of these, with the small unmyelinated fibres actually blocked *first* at low concentrations (Gokin et al., 2001; Fink & Cairns, 1984), which is the opposite of the selectivity a nociceptor-targeted intervention would need.

## 2. THE CODE

The conjecture does not require a coding scheme. It requires only that *some* peripheral traffic, of whatever class, is necessary for the maintenance of the pain percept. This is a much weaker claim than HC-1 or HC-2, and it is the right kind of claim to make at this stage.

The relevant evidence for the coding question — whether the pain-relevant information in CRPS is carried by rate, pattern, labelled lines, or population codes — is not addressed by this experiment, and the conjecture should not be read as addressing it. The Haroutounian result is consistent with *any* coding scheme, because the block removes all of them simultaneously.

**Specific physiology:** In established neuropathic pain, the dorsal horn shows expanded receptive fields, reduced inhibitory tone (loss of KCC2-mediated chloride gradient, Coull et al., 2003), and Aβ-fibre-driven polysynaptic input to lamina I projection neurons (Torsney & MacDermott, 2006; Schoffnegger et al., 2008). The "code" for allodynia is a *circuit* property, not a fibre property. A trunk block tests the circuit's dependence on input, not the code.

---

## 3. WHERE IS PAIN MADE

This is where the conjecture is most honest and most vulnerable.

The conjecture states, correctly, that the discrimination between nociceptive and innocuous traffic is made centrally. It does not claim that a peripheral block *reads* pain. It claims that a peripheral block *removes the substrate* on which the central machinery operates.

That is a defensible position, and it is supported by a substantial body of work. The dorsal horn's sensitised state is maintained by ongoing afferent drive. Remove the drive and the state decays. This has been shown directly in animal models: peripheral nerve block or section after the establishment of central sensitisation reverses the behavioural allodynia and the expanded receptive fields (Pitcher & Henry, 2008; Xie et al., 2005). The central changes are real, but they are not autonomous in the sense of being self-sustaining without input.

**However**, the conjecture's strongest version — "established CRPS is therefore maintained by ongoing primary afferent input, not autonomously generated in the central nervous system" — overstates what the experiment can show. A positive result shows that the pain is *dependent on* peripheral input at the moment of the block. It does not show that the central changes are not autonomous in any meaningful sense. A system can be input-dependent and still be centrally pathological: the input may be normal, and the pathology may be entirely in the gain. The conjecture's own mechanism section acknowledges this, but the Claim does not.

**Specific physiology:** The dorsal horn in established neuropathic pain shows *de novo* expression of genes, altered glial function, and loss of inhibitory interneurons (Scholz & Woolf, 2007; Tsuda et al., 2005). These are central changes. The question the experiment answers is whether they are *sufficient* to maintain pain without peripheral input, not whether they exist. The conjecture should be framed as testing sufficiency, not existence.

## 4. THE PATHOLOGY

The conjecture does reckon with the pathology, but incompletely.

It acknowledges that in established allodynia the traffic that hurts arrives on Aβ fibres. It acknowledges that the central changes are real. What it does not reckon with is the **ectopic spontaneous discharge from the dorsal root ganglion** as a source of ongoing input that a *trunk* block would not remove.

This is a critical point. In CRPS, and in neuropathic pain generally, a substantial fraction of the ongoing afferent drive originates at the **dorsal root ganglion**, not at the peripheral terminals. Ectopic discharge from the DRG has been recorded directly in animal models of neuropathic pain (Wall & Devor, 1983; Kajander et al., 1992; Liu et al., 2000) and is the rationale for DRG stimulation as a therapy (Deer et al., 2017). A peripheral nerve block at the trunk level blocks conduction *distal* to the DRG but does not silence the ectopic generator. The ectopic activity continues to invade the DRG and the dorsal root entry zone.

Does this matter for the experiment? It depends on the site of the block. If the block is proximal enough — at the level of the plexus or the nerve root — it may interrupt conduction from the DRG to the cord. But a trunk-level block of the sciatic or median nerve does not. The conjecture specifies "ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve block proximal to the affected region," which is ambiguous. If the block is at the ankle or wrist, it leaves DRG ectopic activity untouched. If it is at the plexus, it may interrupt it.

**This is a design flaw, not a fatal objection.** The conjecture should specify that the block must be proximal to the DRG, or at least acknowledge that a distal block tests only the contribution of peripheral-terminal and nerve-trunk generators, not DRG ectopic generators. The Haroutounian study used blocks at the level of the sciatic or femoral nerve, which are proximal to the foot but distal to the DRG. The same limitation applies.

**Specific physiology:** Ectopic discharge from the DRG in neuropathic pain is driven by altered expression of voltage-gated sodium channels (Nav1.3, Nav1.7, Nav1.8) and potassium channels (Kv1.1, Kv1.2) in the ganglion (Waxman et al., 1999; Devor, 2006). This discharge is not dependent on peripheral terminals and would not be silenced by a trunk block.

## 5. PLASTICITY AND HABITUATION

The conjecture proposes a single, acute intervention — a nerve block lasting 2 to 8 hours. Plasticity and habituation are not relevant to the primary outcome, which is measured at 30 minutes.

However, the conjecture's "What it would change" section proposes that a positive result would make the peripheral nerve block "a diagnostic test that tells an individual patient whether their pain has a peripheral driver." This is a *repeated* intervention, and here plasticity matters.

Repeated nerve blocks are not a neutral diagnostic. Local anaesthetics have been shown to produce **prolonged analgesia** after a single block in some neuropathic pain conditions (Arner et al., 1990), and repeated blocks can produce **cumulative benefit** or **tachyphylaxis** depending on the agent and the condition. The conjecture should not assume that the diagnostic test is repeatable without consequence.

**Specific physiology:** Systemic lidocaine produces analgesia in neuropathic pain at plasma concentrations that do not block peripheral conduction (Wallace et al., 1996; Attal et al., 2000), which is why the intravenous control arm is essential. The conjecture includes this control, which is correct.

---

## 6. COLLATERAL TRAFFIC

The intervention is a standard peripheral nerve block. The collateral traffic lost during the block is well characterised: touch, proprioception, thermoregulation, sudomotor and vasomotor autonomic function, and motor function in the territory of the blocked nerve.

For a diagnostic test lasting 2 to 8 hours, this is acceptable. For a *therapeutic* intervention repeated over months, it is not, and the conjecture should not be read as proposing that.

The conjecture's Rivals section acknowledges that "the disabling features of CRPS include motor, autonomic and trophic signs that a 4-hour block cannot address." This is correct and should be retained.

**Specific physiology:** A sciatic nerve block produces foot drop and loss of plantar sensation, which is a fall risk. A brachial plexus block produces a flail arm. These are not trivial collateral effects, and they limit the use of the block as a repeated diagnostic.

---

## 7. SPECIES AND PREPARATION

The conjecture's primary support is **human and awake**: Haroutounian et al. (2014) is a human study with quantitative sensory testing and plasma concentration measurement. This is the right kind of evidence for the claim being made.

The animal evidence that supports the mechanism — that central sensitisation is input-dependent — is **rodent, anaesthetised or awake-behaving, and acute or subacute**. The translation from rodent to human for this specific question is reasonably good, because the phenomenon (input-dependent central sensitisation) is robust across species and preparations. But the conjecture should be explicit that the *human* evidence for CRPS specifically is a narrative review and a single case report, and that the Haroutounian study did not include CRPS patients.

**Specific physiology:** The rodent models of neuropathic pain that show input-dependent central sensitisation (CCI, SNI, SNL) are all models of *nerve injury*, not CRPS. CRPS has a different pathophysiology, including a prominent inflammatory component and autonomic dysfunction, that is not captured by these models. The translation from nerve-injury models to CRPS is not automatic.

## Summary of objections

1. **The block is not selective.** It silences Aβ, Aδ, C, and autonomic fibres simultaneously. A positive result cannot tell you which afferent class maintains the pain, and therefore cannot bear on HC-2 or on the nociceptor-targeted intervention programme.

2. **The block site is ambiguous.** A distal block leaves DRG ectopic discharge untouched. The conjecture must specify a block proximal to the DRG, or acknowledge the limitation.

3. **The Claim overstates what the experiment can show.** A positive result shows input-dependence, not absence of central autonomy. The central changes are real and may be necessary even if not sufficient.

4. **The diagnostic-reallocation rival is not adequately handled.** The re-adjudication before the block is good, but the conjecture should pre-specify how it will report the block response separately for patients with and without an identifiable discrete lesion.

5. **The "restore HC-2" claim is unsupported.** A trunk block cannot bear on the question of whether pain-relevant structure is readable from the periphery, because it removes all structure, not just the pain-relevant component.

---

VERDICT: MINOR — Specify the block site relative to the DRG, and remove the claim that a positive result bears on HC-2; the experiment tests input-dependence, not peripheral readability.
