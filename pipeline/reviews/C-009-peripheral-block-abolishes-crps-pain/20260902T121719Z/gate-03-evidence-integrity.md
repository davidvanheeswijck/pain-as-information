### Evidence Base Audit

#### 1. WHAT IS MISSING
A hostile expert would immediately challenge the absence of the following three bodies of literature:
*   **Evidence of Central Autonomy in Long-Standing CRPS:** The conjecture assumes that central sensitization always requires a peripheral trigger. It fails to cite neuroimaging and clinical studies showing autonomous central pain-generator activity or permanent cortical reorganization in late-stage CRPS (e.g., Maihöfner et al., 2003, *Brain*, which shows cortical reorganization correlates with pain intensity and does not immediately reverse with transient peripheral blocks).
*   **The Sympathetic-Sensory Coupling Literature:** CRPS has historically been treated with sympathetic blocks (e.g., stellate ganglion or lumbar sympathetic blocks). The literature on the failure or highly variable placebo-prone response rates of these blocks (e.g., Cepeda et al., 2002/2005 Cochrane reviews) is highly relevant to whether "blocking the input" successfully abolishes CRPS pain.
*   **Small-Fiber Pathology vs. Active Ectopic Generators:** The conjecture assumes CRPS is maintained by active ectopic discharge from primary afferents. It ignores the literature showing that CRPS-I is associated with a *loss* of small-diameter nociceptive fibers (e.g., Oaklander et al., 2006, *Pain*, "Evidence of focal small-fiber axonal degeneration in CRPS-I"), which points to a degenerative pathology rather than a hyperactive peripheral generator.

#### 2. NEGATIVE RESULTS
The conjecture is built almost entirely on a single positive trial in a non-CRPS population (Haroutounian 2014, peripheral nerve injury) and a single positive $n=1$ case report (Kato 2013). It does not engage with the extensive history of failed, unblinded, or open-label regional anesthetic interventions in CRPS. It acknowledges that a nerve block cannot be fully blinded due to loss of sensation, but it fails to engage with the high placebo response rate documented in sham-controlled trials of invasive pain interventions.
