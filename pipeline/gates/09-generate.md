You are generating new conjectures for a research programme on whether chronic
neuropathic pain can be read and rewritten as a signal rather than silenced as
a channel.

You are given: the evidence base, the programme's hard core and branches, the
open ledger, and the refuted ledger. Read the refuted ledger first and read it
properly. Proposing something already in it is the failure that wastes the most
of this programme's time, and it will happen unless you actively guard against
it, because the same priors that produced the dead idea are the ones you are
generating from.

## What a good conjecture looks like here

The best conjectures in this programme come from three places, in descending
order of yield:

1. **A contradiction in the evidence base.** Two well established findings that
   do not sit together. Find one, state it sharply, and propose the resolution.
   This is the highest-value move available to you and you should try it first.
2. **An established capability that is not being used for this.** A method
   routine in another field (cardiac electrophysiology, cochlear implants,
   responsive neurostimulation for epilepsy, telecoms, radar, machine
   listening) that maps onto an open problem here. Say what the mapping is and
   where it breaks.
3. **A measurement nobody has made.** Often the cheapest and most useful. The
   evidence base flags several. If a number that everyone assumes has in fact
   never been measured, proposing to measure it is a legitimate conjecture.

## What a bad conjecture looks like

Do not produce any of these. They will be rejected at triage and they cost the
programme a round.

- A restatement of an established finding as though it were a hypothesis.
- A claim with no forbidden observation, however elaborate.
- "X may play a role in Y." Nothing is at stake in it.
- A mechanism reached for because it is fashionable rather than because the
  evidence points at it. In this programme that means: do not invoke anything
  quantum unless the evidence base gives you an actual reason, and if you do,
  supply the decoherence estimate yourself in the conjecture rather than
  leaving it to the gate.
- A conjecture whose only test is a large randomised trial.
- A conjecture that requires the programme's hard core to be true in order to
  be interesting. Those are not tests, they are elaborations.

## Required output

Produce three conjectures, each in the repository's template format, each
independent of the others, and each from a different one of the three sources
above.

For each, before the template, give two lines:

- **Provenance.** Which specific evidence-base claims generated this, cited by
  their identifiers. A conjecture you cannot trace to the evidence base is one
  you invented, and it should be marked as such honestly rather than dressed in
  borrowed citations.
- **Why not already done.** One sentence, and it must be a real reason. "Nobody
  thought of it" is almost never true and is a sign you have not searched. The
  real reasons are usually: the measurement was not possible until recently,
  the two fields do not talk, the result would be unpublishable if negative, or
  it is not fundable.

Then rank your three by expected information gain per euro, and say which one
you would fund if you could fund only one, and why.

Finally, and this is required: name the **one thing you were tempted to propose
and did not**, and say what stopped you. If nothing stopped you, you were not
being careful.
